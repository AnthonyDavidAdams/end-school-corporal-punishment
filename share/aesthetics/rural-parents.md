# Rural parents in paddling districts

**Where they see images:** Facebook community groups ("[County] Community Chat", "[Town] Swap and Sell", the school's own page), the church bulletin board, the feed store counter, the Friday-night football program, the county fair, text threads from other parents.

**Visual language they trust:** the football program and the fair poster. Heavy slab serifs and condensed grotesques (Rockwell, Impact-adjacent but cleaner, Arial Narrow Bold), navy and cardinal and gold in school colors, a single strong photograph or a flat object on a textured background (kraft paper, chalkboard, denim, plywood), thick rules, all-caps headlines with a sentence-case sub. The best-liked posts in these groups are a photo of a real thing plus a plain sentence. Hand-lettered signboard and stencil type read as local and honest; glossy gradients and stock-photo diversity panels read as outside money.

**What reads as an outsider:** the words "human rights," "UN," "trauma-informed," "developed nations"; purple or teal palettes; sans-serif minimalism; anything that looks like a nonprofit's annual report; sarcasm; lecturing.

**Copy voice:** short, plain, first person, about their own child and their own school. "Your say" and "your kid" beat "students." Numbers are local when possible (the district's own count from `data/crdc/`), state-level otherwise, always with the year. The ask is the form: "You can say no in writing. Here is the letter." Second ask: "Ask the board for the number."

**Formats:** 1080x1080 Facebook square; 1080x1350 portrait; 8.5x11 flyer for the pickup line and the feed store; 4x6 card for the football program.

**Concepts that fit:**
- The paddle as an object on a workbench, alone, with the sentence "Your school still owns one of these. You can say no in writing."
- A school-colors program-style card: "[COUNTY] SCHOOLS, 2021-22: [N] KIDS PADDLED. ASK THE BOARD FOR THIS YEAR'S NUMBER."
- Chalkboard with a plain sentence: "33 states stopped. Their schools are fine. Ours can be too."
- The opt-out letter itself, photographed on a kitchen table, "Print it. Sign it. Hand it to the office."
