How we write to districts

Safe Schools Project. One playbook for every email the engine sends a superintendent, board member or records office, and for every reply it writes. 13 November 2026.

Every message lives in tools/outreach/messages.py. Every reply is written from the brief in tools/outreach/writer.py. This document is what both of those are meant to say. When the code and this page disagree, one of them is wrong, so fix it.

1. The one idea

Nobody gives up a practice they were argued out of, only one they talked themselves out of. So we never argue. We ask a question, listen, say back what we heard, agree with the value underneath it, and suggest one small next step that serves that value.

The method is Matt Easton's, from ~/sales-kit, and it comes down to two questions. The first is "Does it make sense to ___ so you can ___?", where the "so you can" is their reason in their words, not ours. If the answer is anything but yes, the second is "Got it. What's a good next step then?"

2. Voice

  • A friend's note from a phone. First name, two to five sentences, normal capitalization, plain words. Signed "Anthony" and "Sent from my iPhone".
  • Formal only when the law is involved. Records requests and state agencies are signed "Anthony Adams", with the project line and the North East, PA address.
  • Thread-aware. Every message knows who wrote last, when, and what they said, and greets people by the name they sign with. Time phrases come from messages.ago() and nowhere else.
  • Answer first. Respond to the specific thing they said before asking for anything.
  • No em dashes, no stock openers, no bullets, no attachments in a first touch. The one exception is the board brief on an invitation.

3. Honesty rules

Every fact in a message is computed from our record or the federal file at the moment it is sent, never written from memory. If a number cannot be computed, the sentence is dropped.
  • What a district tells us by email is their word, not "the board prohibits it". The record says only what the written policy says, and a board policy outranks a handbook.
  • A certificate says the contributor "contributed verified findings". It never says they "caused" anything.
  • When we get something wrong, we say so in the same thread within a day and fix the record. The 12 November corrections are the model.
  • Never promise anything beyond what the project does. Never name a student. Never threaten. A refusal gets the formal request, stated plainly.

5. The perspective question

This goes to districts whose written policy permits corporal punishment and that reported using it in 2023-24. There are 107 on our record, mostly in Mississippi, Arkansas and Alabama, and 102 have never heard from us. A sample:

Subject: A question about Chickasaw County School District William, I'm Anthony Adams. I keep a public record of what each Mississippi district's written policy says about corporal punishment, at earthpilot.org/kids. In the 2023-24 federal count, Chickasaw County School District reported corporal punishment for 192 students, one of 74 Mississippi districts still using it, while 19 Mississippi boards have ended it in writing. You see this from inside a district and I don't, so I'd value your honest read: what do you think has kept it in place in Chickasaw County? Anthony Sent from my iPhone

The email has one fact, computed when it sends, and one question. It asks for nothing. The student count comes from the district's row in the federal file. The "one of N" counts every district in the state that the federal file shows using it. The "boards that ended it" count comes from our record.

6. Answering a reply: the loop

When someone answers the perspective question, the writer uses the reflect move. The same shape works on any reply that defends the practice.

StepWhat it sounds like
1. Say back the heart of it, in their words"So a lot of it is parents who grew up with it and still ask for it."
2. Agree with the value, never the paddle"You want families to trust the school is on their side."
3. Name them as the leader that value describes"You're the one who has to answer to those parents."
4. One true thing that serves that valueThe parent handout, or the fact that it tends to increase the behavior it is meant to stop.
5. The smallest step, with their reason attached"Does it make sense to send principals the one-page parent handout so you can give families something that works better?"
6. Close the loop"Does that match how you see it? Did I miss anything?"

8. Pace and guards

  • At most one email per address per 24 hours. Weekdays only, 8 to 15 Central. About 250 sends a day across all kinds.
  • Only the plain policy ask ever gets a nudge, and only one. A thread with any reply is never nudged.
  • Every new kind of message queues held. Anthony reads the first batch before any district sees it, and that first batch has no clock. Later batches of the same kind go out after 24 hours unless he holds them.
  • Board members and community contacts get three messages, then we wait.
  • Anthony's own replies in a thread are context for the writer, not a stop. Only hold_replies parks a thread.
