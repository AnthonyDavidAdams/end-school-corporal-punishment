THE SAFE DISCIPLINE PROGRAM
A kit for school districts ending corporal punishment, by EarthPilot. earthpilot.org/kids


CHAPTER: The Safe Discipline Program kit

Everything a district needs to end corporal punishment well: the policy, the motion, the words for
staff, families and students, the training, and the ninety days between the vote and the change.

Offered as an **in-kind grant funded and administered by EarthPilot.org** — no cost to the district,
no cash changes hands. Delivered with coaching for the superintendent and principals through the
first year, including the board presentation and the parent letter.

The position is the best one, not the easiest one: **prohibited entirely.** Not "with parental
consent," not "as a last resort." The evidence base (`../training/evidence.md`), the American Academy
of Pediatrics (2023), the Secretary of Education (2023), 137 of 199 countries, and every district in
Kentucky and North Carolina are on that side. A district that adopts consent-only is choosing to keep
hitting the children whose parents say yes, and the data say those are disproportionately the
children with the least protection.

and the alternatives are Modules 2 through 5: classroom routines, the office response ladder, repair
conversations, and plans for the few students who keep coming back.

## Three rules for using it

1. **Say it was a mistake.** Not "times have changed." The announcements are written to acknowledge,
   without excuse, that the district hit children and should not have. Districts that skip this
   spend the next year relitigating it; districts that say it once are done with it.
2. **Never name a child, a family, or an employee** in any of these documents or in any meeting
   about them. The history is institutional.
3. **Cite only what is in `../facts/claims/`.** Every figure in the kit carries the id of the claim
   it comes from. Check `../facts/README.md` before printing; numbers move.

## Where the words came from

The acknowledgment language follows the districts and countries that ended the practice and said
so plainly rather than quietly (`intl-comparators-how-they-ended`,
`campaign-north-carolina-all-districts-2018`, `campaign-kentucky-zero-2023-24`). The alternatives
are the ones with randomized evidence (`alt-pbis-bradshaw-rcts`, `alt-restorative-rand-2018`,
`alt-good-behavior-game-kellam`), stated with their limits.


CHAPTER: Model policy: prohibition of corporal punishment

A complete board policy, written so a solicitor can adopt it with the district's numbering and
nothing else. Placeholders are in [BRACKETS]. The policy does one thing — ends the practice — and
then says what happens instead, because a prohibition with nothing behind it produces a suspension
spike and a board that reverses itself (`../training/modules/01-transition-plan.md`).

**State association numbering, so it lands in the right slot of the manual:** Arkansas ASBA 4.39 ·
Missouri MSBA JGA / JGA-1 · Oklahoma FO · Mississippi and Alabama JDB · Georgia JDA · Texas FO(LOCAL)
· Tennessee TSBA 6.314. Replace the existing policy under that code; do not add a second one beside it.

```
POLICY [NUMBER]: CORPORAL PUNISHMENT

[DISTRICT NAME] BOARD OF EDUCATION
Adopted: [DATE]          Effective: [DATE, ninety days after adoption]
Rescinds: Policy [NUMBER] of [DATE]

1. PURPOSE

The Board's purpose is a safe, orderly school in which every student is present, learning, and
treated with dignity. Discipline exists to teach and to keep people safe. The Board finds that the
deliberate infliction of physical pain on a student does neither, and ends its use.

2. DEFINITIONS

Corporal punishment means the deliberate infliction of physical pain on a student as a means of
discipline, correction or control, including hitting, paddling, spanking, slapping, shaking,
pinching, striking with any object, forcing a student to hold a painful position, or denying a
student food, water, or use of a restroom as a consequence.

Corporal punishment does not mean the reasonable physical force that is necessary, and no more
than is necessary, to stop a student from injuring themselves or another person, to separate
students who are fighting, to take a weapon or dangerous object from a student, or to protect
property from serious damage, when no less intrusive means is available and the force ends when
the danger ends. Such force is governed by the district's policy on physical restraint and by
state law, and every use is documented and reported under Section 7.

3. PROHIBITION

No employee, contractor, volunteer, or other agent of the district shall administer corporal
punishment to any student, in any school, program, vehicle or activity of the district, for any
reason, at any time. No parent or guardian may authorize it. No student may consent to it or
choose it in place of another consequence.

4. WHAT REPLACES IT

The Superintendent shall implement, in every school, a published system of positive behavior
supports and graduated responses to misbehavior that:

 (a) states three to five expectations for student conduct, taught and re-taught in every classroom;
 (b) sets out a graduated ladder of office responses, published in the student handbook and posted
     in every classroom, in which every response returns the student to class and requires a repair
     of the harm done, and in which exclusion from school is the last rung, reserved for threats to
     safety;
 (c) provides a plan, with a daily adult contact, for any student who receives three office
     referrals in a month;
 (d) is applied the same way by every administrator in the district.

5. TRAINING

Before the effective date, every employee who supervises students shall complete the district's
training in classroom routines, the office response ladder, and repair conversations. New
employees shall complete it within sixty days of hire. The Superintendent shall certify completion
to the Board.

6. FAMILIES

The Superintendent shall notify every family in writing of this policy before its effective date,
in the family's language, including the office response ladder and how to raise a concern. No
opt-out, consent form, or waiver concerning corporal punishment shall be distributed or accepted.

7. RECORDS AND REPORTING

Every office referral shall be logged with the date, the behavior, the response given, and the
student's grade, race, sex, disability status and English-learner status. The Superintendent shall
report to the Board, and publish, each quarter: the number of referrals and of each response by
school; the number and days of any exclusion from school; the regular-attendance rate; and the
same figures by student group. Any use of physical force under Section 2 shall be documented the
same day and reported to the Superintendent and the student's parent or guardian within
twenty-four hours.

8. VIOLATIONS

An employee who administers corporal punishment in violation of this policy is subject to
discipline up to and including dismissal, and the incident shall be reported as required by law,
including to the Department of Children and Families or its equivalent where the law requires.
An employee who reports a violation in good faith shall not be retaliated against.

9. REVIEW

The Board shall review the reports under Section 7 annually, in public session, and shall review
this policy after one year and every three years thereafter.

Legal references: [state statute permitting or governing corporal punishment, cited so the record
shows the Board acted within it]; [state restraint and seclusion law]; 20 U.S.C. 1400 et seq.;
[state child abuse reporting law].
```

## Notes for the solicitor

- **Rescission, not amendment.** Replacing the old policy under its own number removes the
  authorizing language entirely. An amendment that leaves "may be administered" anywhere in the
  manual leaves the practice alive in the handbook a year later.
- **Section 2 is the paragraph that gets attacked.** It is deliberately narrower than the state
  restraint statute, and it names the four situations, because "reasonable force to maintain
  order" is how the practice survives a ban. Keep the four.
- **Section 3's last two sentences** close the two doors districts most often leave open: parental
  authorization and the student's "choice" of licks over detention. Both are how the practice
  continues in consent-only districts, and the children who "choose" it are overwhelmingly the
  ones with the least power to refuse.
- **Section 7 is the policy's teeth.** A board that publishes referral and exclusion numbers by
  group every quarter does not drift back. The data fields match the federal Civil Rights Data
  Collection, so the district's own report and its federal filing will agree.
- **Effective date at ninety days** gives the training in Section 5 time to happen first. The
  transition plan is `../training/modules/01-transition-plan.md`.


CHAPTER: The motion and the resolution

Two documents. The **motion** is what a member says at the meeting; it is short because it is
spoken. The **resolution** is what the record keeps; every "whereas" carries the id of the verified
claim behind it (`../facts/claims/`), so a member challenged on any line can produce the source.

## The motion

> I move that the Board adopt Policy [NUMBER], Corporal Punishment, as presented on first reading,
> which rescinds Policy [NUMBER] of [DATE]; that the policy take effect on [DATE], ninety days from
> today; and that the Superintendent be directed to complete the staff training and the family
> notification before that date and to report to the Board at each regular meeting until then.

If the district requires two readings, say "as presented on first reading" and bring the resolution
at the second. If a member asks for consent-only instead, the answer is in
[01-model-policy.md](01-model-policy.md), Section 3, notes.

## The resolution

```
RESOLUTION No. [NUMBER]
OF THE [DISTRICT NAME] BOARD OF EDUCATION
ENDING CORPORAL PUNISHMENT

WHEREAS, this district reported to the United States Department of Education that [N] of its
students received corporal punishment in the 2023-24 school year (crdc-national-total-2023-24-computed;
the district's own figure is in the record at earthpilot.org/kids/state/[XX]/); and

WHEREAS, 19,851 public school students in the United States received corporal punishment in
2023-24 by the Department's own count, which the Department has called likely underreported
(crdc-national-total-2023-24-computed; crdc-undercount-caveat); and

WHEREAS, in a propensity-matched study of more than 800 young adults from the states where school
corporal punishment is legal, those who had received it had lower high-school grade point averages
and more depressive symptoms (study-gershoff-sattler-holden-2019), and a 2022 meta-analysis of the
school-specific research found it associated with worse developmental outcomes
(study-visser-2022-school-meta-analysis); and

WHEREAS, Black students were 26 percent of students receiving corporal punishment in 2021-22 and 15
percent of enrollment, and students with disabilities have been struck at rates above their share of
enrollment in every year measured (crdc-black-students-share-2021-22; crdc-disability-share); and

WHEREAS, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended in August 2023 that corporal punishment in
all school settings be abolished and replaced by alternative forms of behavior management
(org-aap-2023-school-statement), and the Secretary of Education wrote to every governor and chief
state school officer in March 2023 asking the states to end it (law-cardona-letter-2023); and

WHEREAS, corporal punishment is fully prohibited in schools in 137 of the 199 countries and
territories tracked (intl-schools-prohibited-137-of-199), every one of North Carolina's 115
districts ended it by policy by 2018 (campaign-north-carolina-all-districts-2018), and every one of
Kentucky's 171 districts ended it by 2023, after which the state recorded zero instances
(campaign-kentucky-zero-2023-24); and

WHEREAS, school-wide positive behavior supports reduced office referrals and suspensions in a
five-year randomized trial across 37 schools (alt-pbis-bradshaw-rcts), and the training to
implement them is available to this district at no cost; and

WHEREAS, the Board finds that the practice does not serve the district's stated goals for
attendance, achievement, or a climate in which every student is treated with dignity;

NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED that the Board of Education of [DISTRICT NAME]:

1. Adopts Policy [NUMBER], Corporal Punishment, prohibiting the practice in every school,
   program, vehicle and activity of the district, and rescinds Policy [NUMBER] of [DATE];

2. Sets the effective date at [DATE], ninety days from adoption, and directs the Superintendent to
   complete the training of every employee who supervises students, and the notification of every
   family, before that date;

3. Directs the Superintendent to implement the graduated system of responses described in the
   policy in every school by the effective date and to publish it in the student handbook;

4. Directs the Superintendent to report office referrals, responses, exclusions and the
   regular-attendance rate, by school and by student group, to the Board in public session each
   quarter, beginning with the quarter before the effective date so that a baseline is on the record;

5. Acknowledges that the district's past use of the practice was a mistake, and directs that this
   acknowledgment be included in the district's communication to staff, families and students;

6. Directs the Superintendent to review this policy with the Board after one year.

ADOPTED this [DAY] day of [MONTH], [YEAR], by a vote of [AYES] to [NAYS].

_______________________________        _______________________________
Board President                        Board Secretary
```

## If the vote is close

Clause 5 is the one members will want to strike. Keep it. The districts that reversed themselves
are the ones that ended the practice as a procedural change and left the belief in place; the
acknowledgment is what makes the change hold in the building. The Commons in 1986 and the Kentucky
board in 2023 both said it plainly (`intl-comparators-how-they-ended`, `campaign-kentucky-zero-2023-24`).


CHAPTER: Announcing the change to staff

Staff hear it first, in person, before families and before students. A teacher who learns from a
parent that the paddle is gone has been told she does not matter, and she will say so in the
teachers' lounge for a year. The order is: leadership team the day of the vote, all staff within
the week, families the same week, students the week after.

Two documents: the superintendent's message to every employee, and the script for the all-staff
meeting each principal runs.

## The message from the superintendent

> **To every employee of [DISTRICT NAME]**
>
> On [DATE] the Board voted to end corporal punishment in our schools. The policy takes effect on
> [DATE]. Between now and then, this is what changes, and why.
>
> **First, the honest part.** For as long as any of us have worked here, this district has hit
> children as a form of discipline. Last year we reported [N] students to the federal government
> under that heading. Most of us did it, or sent a student to have it done, or knew it was
> happening and said nothing, because it was the policy and it was the custom and it was what was
> done to us. It was a mistake. Not a mistake of the people who carried it out — a mistake of the
> institution that asked them to. The Board has said so in its resolution, and I am saying so to
> you before I say it to anyone else.
>
> **The principle is simple.** No one, ever, deserves to be hit — not for any reason, not by anyone
> whose job is to care for them. We would not accept it for ourselves in this workplace. We are
> done accepting it for children.
>
> **We are late, not early.** The Supreme Court left this to the states in 1977. The United
> Kingdom ended it in state schools in 1986, New Zealand in 1990, Canada in 2004. It is prohibited
> in schools in 137 of the 199 countries that are tracked. Every district in North Carolina had
> ended it by 2018 and every district in Kentucky by 2023; Kentucky then recorded zero instances.
> The American Academy of Pediatrics asked every state to end it in 2023 and the Secretary of
> Education wrote to every governor the same year. We are among the last, and we are choosing not
> to be the very last.
>
> **What replaces it is not nothing.** The threat of the paddle was doing work in our classrooms,
> and we are not pretending otherwise. Over the next ninety days every one of us will be trained
> in what does that work instead: the classroom routines that prevent most referrals, the office
> response ladder that every administrator will use the same way, and the five-minute repair
> conversation that makes a consequence mean something. These are not new ideas from outside; they
> are what the trials show works, and they are what the districts that ended the practice before us
> use. The training is [DATES]. It is on paid time.
>
> **What you will be asked to do.** Teach the expectations. Use the ladder. Log the referral. Have
> the repair conversation. Ask for help with the student who keeps coming back, because there is a
> plan for that student and it is not the paddle. What you will not be asked to do is manage a
> classroom without support: the leadership team will read the referral log every week and fix the
> hallway, the schedule or the transition before it looks at the teacher.
>
> **We can all change.** Many of you were paddled as students and turned out fine, and you will be
> asked about that. The answer is that we turned out fine because of the adults who cared about us,
> not because of the board, and that we can be those adults without it. I include myself in every
> sentence of this letter.
>
> Questions come to me directly at [EMAIL] or to your principal. The full policy is at [LINK].
>
> [NAME], Superintendent

## The all-staff meeting script

*Forty minutes. Principal speaks; leadership team present. No slides for the first ten minutes.*

**Minutes 0-10. Say it.** Read the second and third paragraphs of the superintendent's message
aloud, or say them in your own words. Then stop and let the room be quiet. Do not fill the silence
with the training schedule. Someone will speak; usually it is a veteran, and usually what they say
is some version of "about time" or "I never liked doing it." Let them.

**Minutes 10-20. The history, in one page.** Hand out [06-history.md](06-history.md). Walk through
the six dates. The point is not to shame the district; it is to show that the change is ordinary
and that everyone who made it before us is still running schools.

**Minutes 20-35. What happens instead.** Put the office response ladder on the wall
(`../training/modules/03-office-response-ladder.md`). Walk one referral up it: a student who cussed
a teacher. Rung 1 on Monday, rung 2 on the second time, what the repair task is, who calls the
parent. Then the question everyone is thinking: "And if that doesn't work?" Answer with rung 4 and
Module 5 — the plan, the daily check-in, the weekly review — and be honest that the trials say
referrals go *up* in month one as staff refer what they used to handle with a threat, and come
down in month two if the ladder is used as written.

**Minutes 35-40. The three things.** Training dates (paid). What to say to a student who asks
(see [05-announcement-students.md](05-announcement-students.md), "when a student asks you").
Where to bring a concern: to you, in person, and it will be answered.

**What not to do in this meeting:** name any student or family; name any employee who administered
it; say "the state is making us" (it is not, and staff know it); apologize for the change.

## The three questions staff ask, and the answers

**"I was paddled and I'm fine."** "So was I. We were fine because of the adults who cared about
us. We can be those adults without the board. And the research on the kids who weren't fine is
now large enough that we don't get to bet on it."

**"The kids will run wild."** "They might, for about a month, if we take the paddle away and put
nothing in its place. That is why the training comes first and the policy takes effect in ninety
days, and why the office ladder is on the wall in every room. The districts that did this before
us saw referrals rise in month one and fall in month two."

**"Some parents want it."** "Some do, and they will be told the same thing we are telling you:
that it was a mistake, and that no one hits their child here, including with their permission.
The parent letter has the words. If a parent wants to talk about it, send them to me."


CHAPTER: The letter to families

Sent to every family, in the family's language, in the same week staff are told and before students
are. Printed and mailed, not only posted; the families most affected are the least likely to be on
the app. The superintendent signs it, not a principal, because the district is the one acknowledging.

Placeholders in [BRACKETS]. Keep it to one page. The facts are from `../facts/claims/`.

> **[DISTRICT NAME] School District**
> **[DATE]**
>
> Dear families,
>
> On [DATE] our Board of Education voted to end corporal punishment — paddling — in all of our
> schools. The change takes effect on [DATE]. I am writing to tell you what is changing, why, and
> what will happen instead.
>
> **What we are acknowledging.** For many years this district has used paddling as a form of
> discipline. Last year we reported to the federal government that [N] of our students received it.
> Some of you received it yourselves as students here. The Board and I have concluded that it was a
> mistake, and we are saying so plainly: no child in this district should have been hit, and no
> child will be again. We are sorry.
>
> **Why now.** The evidence has settled. Students who were paddled in school do worse, on average,
> in school and after it. The American Academy of Pediatrics asked every state to end the practice
> in 2023. Every school district in Kentucky and North Carolina has already ended it. It is
> prohibited in schools in 137 of the 199 countries that are counted. We are not early. We are
> choosing not to be last.
>
> **What happens instead.** Discipline is not going away; the paddle is. Every school will use the
> same published set of responses when a student breaks a rule, from a five-minute reset and a note
> home, to a call you will get from your child in the office with the principal beside them, to a
> meeting with you the same day for anything serious. Every response ends with the student back in
> class and making right whatever they did. The full list is in the student handbook and on the
> wall of every classroom. Suspension is the last step, for safety only, not the second step.
>
> **Every teacher and administrator is being trained** in these responses before the change takes
> effect. That training is why there are ninety days between the vote and the day the policy
> begins.
>
> **You no longer need to opt out.** In the past, some families filed a written objection to keep
> their child from being paddled. That form is no longer needed and will not be offered. No one
> hits your child here, with or without permission. If you had wanted to keep paddling as an
> option, I understand that it comes from wanting your child to behave and to be safe, and I would
> like to talk with you about how we will do that; my number is below.
>
> **What you can do.** Read the handbook page with your child. Tell us when something is not
> working. We will publish, every quarter, how many students were sent to the office, what happened,
> and how many were suspended, so that you can see whether this is working as well as we can.
>
> Thank you for trusting us with your children. We intend to be worthy of it.
>
> [NAME], Superintendent
> [PHONE] · [EMAIL]

## Notes

- The paragraph beginning "If you had wanted to keep paddling" is the one that matters most and the
  one districts most want to cut. It is the difference between a family that feels overruled and a
  family that feels invited. The conversation it offers is in `../training/modules/07-parents.md`
  ("Script: the parent who wanted paddling").
- Attach the office response ladder as the second page, in plain language.
- Send it in every language spoken by more than a handful of families; the district's English-learner
  count is in its own data.


CHAPTER: Telling the students

Students are told last, after staff and families, and they are told in their classroom by the adult
they see every day, not at an assembly by a stranger. Short, honest, and then on with the day.
Nothing about it is a lesson plan; the whole thing is under five minutes.

The three things every version says: **it was a mistake; nobody deserves to be hit; here is what
happens now.** Do not say "we used to" without saying "we were wrong." Children have very good
detectors for an adult who is describing a change without owning it.

## Grades K-2

> "I have something to tell you. In our school, when someone broke a rule, sometimes a grown-up
> would spank them with a paddle. We are not going to do that anymore, starting [DAY]. We decided
> it was wrong. Nobody should be hit — not you, not anybody, not for any reason.
>
> "If you break a rule, here is what happens instead: I will remind you of the rule. If it keeps
> happening, you'll go talk with [PRINCIPAL'S NAME] for a few minutes and then come right back. And
> if you hurt somebody's feelings or broke something, you'll help fix it. That's it. Does anyone
> have a question?"

Answer questions for two minutes. The common one: "What if I'm really bad?" Answer: "Then you and I
and your grown-ups will make a plan to help you. Nobody gets hit."

## Grades 3-5

> "Starting [DAY], nobody in this school gets paddled anymore. Not as a punishment, not for anything.
> The school board voted on it, and they said something I want you to hear: it was a mistake that we
> ever did it. Nobody deserves to be hit. Not you, not your little brother, not anyone. Grown-ups
> can be wrong, and grown-ups can change.
>
> "Rules are still rules. Here's what happens when one is broken." (Walk the first three rungs of
> the ladder on the wall: the reminder, the office reset and note home, the call home you make
> yourself with the principal there and the thing you do to make it right.) "And if the same
> problem keeps happening, we make a plan with you and your family. That's the whole thing."

## Grades 6-8

> "You've probably already heard, so here it is straight. As of [DAY], corporal punishment is over
> in this district — no paddling, no licks, no choosing licks instead of detention. The board voted
> for it and they put a line in the resolution that says the district was wrong to do it. I agree
> with that line. Some of you have been paddled here. I'm not going to ask who. I am going to say
> that you didn't deserve it, and that nobody does, and that the adults here are supposed to be the
> ones who show you that.
>
> "What changes for you: the office ladder on the wall. Same steps for everyone, every
> administrator, every time. Every step ends with you back in class and with you making right
> whatever it was — an apology that's actually to the person, fixing what got broken. Suspension is
> the last step, for safety only. If you keep landing in the office, you'll get a plan and one adult
> who checks in with you every morning. That's not a punishment. That's the part that works.
>
> "One more thing. If anyone — anyone — hits you at school after [DAY], you tell me or any adult
> here, and it will be dealt with. That's the policy and it's the law."

## Grades 9-12

> "As of [DAY], corporal punishment is finished here. The board's resolution says the district's
> past use of it was a mistake, and I'll add that I think so too. Some of you were paddled in this
> building or in the elementary school. You didn't deserve it. Nobody does. There's a lot of
> research on what it does to kids, and there's a lot of history — the Supreme Court punted on this
> in 1977, most of the world ended it decades ago, every district in Kentucky ended it two years ago
> and the state went to zero. We're late. We're done being late.
>
> "Practically: the handbook page on the office ladder is what governs now. Same steps for
> everyone. Every step ends with a repair, not a removal. Suspension is for safety threats, not for
> mouthing off. And the district is going to publish the numbers every quarter — referrals,
> suspensions, by school — so you can hold us to it.
>
> "If you want to talk about any of this, my door is open. If you want to be part of making the
> ladder work in this building, student government is where that happens, and I'd like it to."

## When a student asks you, one on one

Any adult may be asked, in the hallway, "Is it true they can't paddle us anymore?" The answer is
one sentence, and every adult gives the same one:

> "It's true. It was wrong, and it's over. Nobody here gets hit."

If the student was paddled and wants to talk about it, listen, and then walk them to the counselor
or the principal. Do not say "you probably deserved it," "it wasn't that bad," or "I was paddled
and I'm fine." Say: "I'm sorry that happened to you here."

## The handbook page

Replace the corporal punishment section of the student handbook with this, verbatim:

> **Discipline.** Corporal punishment is prohibited in every school and program of this district.
> No employee, volunteer or visitor may hit, paddle, spank or otherwise physically punish a student,
> and no parent or student may give permission for it. When a rule is broken, the school follows the
> office response ladder printed on the next page: every step returns the student to class and asks
> them to repair the harm done, and exclusion from school is the last step, for threats to safety.
> A student who is hit by an adult at school should tell any staff member; the report will be acted
> on and the student will not be retaliated against.


CHAPTER: The history, in one page

For the administrator or teacher who has to answer "why now?" Every line carries the id of the
verified claim in `../facts/claims/`; the sources are there.

**1977.** The United States Supreme Court, 5 to 4, holds in *Ingraham v. Wright* that the Eighth
Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment does not apply to paddling in public schools, and
that a student need not be given a hearing first, because state tort and criminal law are remedy
enough. The Court did not say the practice was good; it said the Constitution did not reach it and
left the question to the states. (`law-ingraham-v-wright-1977`)

**1986.** The United Kingdom ends corporal punishment in state schools by act of Parliament. New
Zealand follows by statute in 1990, Canada by a Supreme Court ruling in 2004, South Korea by
national decree in 2011, Australia state by state. (`intl-comparators-how-they-ended`)

**Today, abroad.** Corporal punishment is fully prohibited in schools in 137 of the 199 states and
territories tracked by the Global Initiative to End All Corporal Punishment of Children.
(`intl-schools-prohibited-137-of-199`)

**Today, here.** It remains legal by statute in 17 states. In two of them, Kentucky and North
Carolina, every district has prohibited it by policy, leaving 15 where it is both legal and in use.
(`law-states-permitting-count`)

**2018.** Graham County becomes the last of North Carolina's 115 districts to vote to end it. The
state law still permits it; no district uses it. (`campaign-north-carolina-all-districts-2018`)

**2023.** In March, the Secretary of Education writes to every governor and chief state school
officer asking the states to end the practice, citing federal data that Black students were 2.3
times as likely as white students to receive it. (`law-cardona-letter-2023`) In August, the
American Academy of Pediatrics recommends it be abolished in all school settings in all states and
replaced with alternative forms of behavior management. (`org-aap-2023-school-statement`) In
November, the last of Kentucky's 171 districts adopts a prohibiting policy; the state records zero
instances in 2023-24. (`campaign-kentucky-zero-2023-24`)

**2023-24.** 19,851 public school students in the United States receive corporal punishment, by the
Department of Education's own count, which the Department calls likely underreported. Black students
are about a quarter of those struck and about 15 percent of enrollment; students with disabilities
are struck above their share of enrollment in every year measured.
(`crdc-national-total-2023-24-computed`, `crdc-undercount-caveat`, `crdc-black-students-share-2021-22`,
`crdc-disability-share`)

**What the research found, in one sentence each.**
Young adults who were paddled in school had lower high-school grades and more depressive symptoms
than matched peers who were not (`study-gershoff-sattler-holden-2019`). A meta-analysis of the
school-specific studies found the practice associated with worse developmental outcomes
(`study-visser-2022-school-meta-analysis`). School-wide positive behavior supports reduced
suspensions and office referrals in a five-year randomized trial across 37 schools
(`alt-pbis-bradshaw-rcts`). Restorative practices cut days lost to suspension by 36 percent
against 18 percent in control schools in a 44-school randomized trial, and narrowed racial
disparities, though middle-grade academic outcomes worsened in that trial — which is why the kit
pairs repair with classroom routines rather than relying on it alone (`alt-restorative-rand-2018`).

**What the teachers' unions said.** The American Federation of Teachers' only resolution on the
subject, from 1976, intervened in *Ingraham* to protect "a teacher's right to maintain discipline
in the classroom." It has never been replaced. (`org-aft-1976-no-opposition`) A district ending the
practice today is ahead of its national union, not behind it.

**The sentence to remember.** We are not early. We are choosing not to be last.


CHAPTER: Training, transition, and what replaces the paddle

The policy ends the practice. This document is the ninety days that make the ending hold, and the
one page a teacher keeps on the desk afterward.

## The training

Ten open modules in `../training/modules/`, about fourteen staff hours per teacher, run by the
principal or a teacher-leader with the facilitator guide (Module 9). No outside trainer is needed;
coaching from the program is available for the facilitator.

| Module | Title | Who | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Why change, in the words of the schools that did | All staff | 1 h |
| 1 | The 90-day transition plan | Leadership team | 2 h to plan |
| 2 | Classroom routines that prevent most referrals | Every teacher and aide | 2 half-days |
| 3 | The office: what happens instead of the paddle | Administrators | 3 h |
| 4 | Repair conversations that work in five minutes | All staff | half-day |
| 5 | The 5 percent: students with repeated referrals | Leadership team, counselor | 3 h + 20 min/week |
| 6 | Disabilities and trauma | All staff | half-day |
| 7 | Parents: the letter, the meeting, the opt-out, the ally | Administrators, office staff | 2 h |
| 8 | Data: the referral log and the monthly review | Leadership team | 2 h |
| 9 | Facilitator guide and slide notes | Whoever runs it | — |

The evidence behind each module is in `../training/evidence.md`, tagged VERIFIED or REPORTED by
whether the primary source was opened.

## The ninety days (Module 1, condensed)

**Days 1-30: prepare.** Adopt the policy with the effective date at day 90. Start the referral log
now, before anything changes, so there is a baseline. Train every teacher in Module 2. Write the
office ladder and post it in every room. Tell staff, then families, then students (kit documents
3, 4, 5).

**Days 31-60: run both systems.** The ladder is the default. Weekly twenty-minute leadership
meeting: read the log, name the top three behaviors, locations and times of day, and fix the
environment first — the hallway, the transition, the schedule — before looking at any teacher.
Train Module 4.

**Days 61-90: switch.** Identify the students with three or more referrals (usually 3 to 5
percent of enrollment) and start a Module 5 plan for each. Train Module 6. On day 90 the policy
takes effect; announce the baseline numbers and commit to publishing the log monthly.

**What to expect.** Referrals go up in month one after the switch, because staff refer what they
used to handle with a threat. That is expected and it is data. They come down in month two if the
ladder is used as written. A suspension spike means the ladder is being skipped; go back to
Module 3, not to the paddle.

## What replaces the paddle: the one page for the desk

**In the classroom (Module 2).** Three to five posted expectations, stated as what to do. Arrivals
and transitions scripted. Five specific positives to every correction. The Good Behavior Game two
or three times a week in K-5. A calm, private, two-step correction: restate the expectation to the
student and move on; then a choice with a consequence you control.

**In the office (Module 3): the ladder.** Same for every administrator, every time.

| Rung | For | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First minor | Re-teach, five-minute reset, back to class, note home |
| 2 | Repeated minor | Reflection sheet, a repair task, the student calls the parent with the administrator present |
| 3 | Third minor in a month, or first major | Same-day parent meeting, repair conversation with the person affected, loss of a named privilege |
| 4 | Repeated major | Behavior plan, in-school placement with work, daily check-in, weekly parent contact |
| 5 | Safety: weapons, serious assault, drugs | Out-of-school suspension per code, re-entry meeting the first day back, a plan |

Nobody skips rungs except for rung 5. Every rung ends with a return to class and a repair. Every
rung is logged.

**The repair (Module 4): five questions.** What happened? What were you thinking at the time? Who
has been affected, and how? What do you need to do to make it right? What will you do differently
next time? The answer to the fourth question is the repair task, and it goes on the form.

**The student who keeps coming back (Module 5).** Three referrals in a month puts a student on the
list. Two weeks of Check-In/Check-Out: two minutes with one chosen adult each morning and afternoon,
a point card between classes, a graph. If the graph is flat, a thirty-minute plan meeting with the
student and the family, and one thing changed at a time, reviewed weekly.

**The student with a disability (Module 6).** Behavior that impedes learning is an IEP-team
question, and positive behavioral supports must be considered; the paddle was never a legal answer
for these students in most of the permitting states and is now not an answer for anyone.

## What gets measured, and published

Referrals and each response by school; exclusions and days lost; the regular-attendance rate; all
of it by student group, every quarter, in public session (policy Section 7). The fields match the
federal Civil Rights Data Collection, so the district's own report and its federal filing agree.
A board that sees the numbers does not drift back.

## Accountability is not gone; it is specific

The paddle asked nothing of a student afterward. The ladder asks something specific of a specific
student to a specific person, and checks that it was done. That is the whole difference, and it is
the one that staff who were sceptical in month one describe, in month three, as the part that
works.


CHAPTER: Module 0: Why change, in the words of the schools that did

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Audience:** school board, full staff on the first in-service day.

## What this module replaces

The assumption that ending corporal punishment means losing control of the building.

## The evidence, briefly

See `../evidence.md` sections A1, A7 and A12. The short version: the meta-analyses find corporal punishment does not even reliably produce immediate compliance (d = .14, not significant) and is associated with more aggression and worse mental health later; the school-specific meta-analysis finds worse behavior and lower achievement; no study has found that ending it increases misbehavior, and no study has found that using it improves behavior. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends abolishing it in every state by law.

## The practice

Read aloud, or play, three accounts from principals in districts like yours that stopped. Sourced accounts are collected in `../../strategy/case-studies/`. Then ask the staff three questions and write the answers on the wall:

1. What do we actually use corporal punishment for? (List the behaviors. It is usually the same five.)
2. Which of those behaviors does paddling stop for good? (It will be none.)
3. What are we afraid of if we stop?

The third list is the training plan. Modules 2 through 5 answer every item on it.

## Handout

A one-page sheet: the district's own numbers from `../../data/crdc/`, the list of neighboring districts that have ended the practice from `../../data/districts/`, and the 2023 AAP school statement in one paragraph (`../../facts/claims/org-aap-2023-school-statement.md`).

## When it is not working

If staff are hostile, do not argue the evidence. Ask the second question again and wait. Every veteran teacher knows the students who were paddled most were paddled repeatedly, which is the whole case.


CHAPTER: Module 1: The 90-day transition plan

**Time:** 2 hours to plan, 90 days to execute. **Audience:** principal, assistant principal, one teacher per grade band, counselor if any.

## What this module replaces

An abrupt policy change with nothing behind it, which is how districts end up with a suspension spike and a board that reverses itself.

## The plan

**Days 1 to 30: prepare.**
- Adopt the board policy (`../../templates/district-policy.md`) with an effective date at day 90.
- Define the office referral form and the referral log (Module 8). Start logging now, before the change, so you have a baseline.
- Train every teacher in Module 2 (classroom routines). Two half-days.
- Write the office response ladder (Module 3) and post it in every classroom.
- Send the parent letter (Module 7).

**Days 31 to 60: run both systems.**
- The ladder is the default. Corporal punishment is not administered without the principal reviewing whether the ladder was used first. Use drops sharply here on its own.
- Weekly 20-minute leadership meeting: read the referral log, name the top three behaviors, the top three locations, the top three times of day. Fix the environment first (hallway supervision, transition routines, the lunch line).
- Train staff in Module 4 (repair conversations). One half-day.

**Days 61 to 90: switch.**
- Identify the students with three or more referrals (usually 3 to 5 percent of enrollment). Start Module 5 plans for each.
- Train on Module 6 (disabilities and trauma).
- Day 90: the policy takes effect. Announce the baseline numbers and commit to publishing the monthly log.

## Hours

Roughly 14 staff hours of training per teacher over 90 days plus 20 minutes a week for the leadership team. Compare against the hours currently spent on paddling paperwork, parent calls, and the occasional lawyer.

## When it is not working

Referrals go up in the first month after the switch in many schools because staff refer what they used to handle with a threat. That is expected and it is data. It comes down in month two if the ladder is used consistently. If it does not, the problem is almost always inconsistent responses across administrators; fix the ladder, not the policy.


CHAPTER: Module 2: Classroom routines that prevent most referrals

**Time:** two half-days of training plus 20 minutes a week of coaching for the first semester. **Audience:** every teacher and aide.

## What this module replaces

The threat. In a paddling school, most classroom management runs on the possibility of being sent to the office. Remove the paddle without replacing the threat with a routine and referrals climb. This module is the routine.

## The evidence

- Classroom management programs improve behavior, academics and social-emotional outcomes with an average effect of g = 0.22 across 54 controlled studies (`../evidence.md` B5, Korpershoek et al. 2016).
- The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide's two strong-evidence recommendations are: modify the classroom environment, and teach and reinforce new skills (B10).
- The Good Behavior Game, a free classroom routine, produced effects still measurable at ages 19 to 21 in randomized trials (B3, Kellam et al.).
- Teacher classroom-management training (Incredible Years) works in randomized trials, with small average effects that are larger for the students who start furthest behind (B5, Reinke et al. 2018).

## The practice: five routines

1. **Three to five posted expectations,** stated as what to do ("Hands and feet to yourself," "Voice level 1 during work"), taught in the first week with examples and practice, and re-taught after every break. The same expectations in every room and hallway in the building; that is the whole-school part (`03-office-response-ladder.md`).
2. **Transitions and arrivals scripted.** Most referrals come from the first five minutes, the last five, and the hallway. Write down what students do on entry (a task on the board, materials out), how the class moves, and how it ends. Practice it like a fire drill.
3. **Five positives to one correction.** Specific, quiet, and frequent: "Marcus, you started right away." The ratio is the single most replicated finding in the classroom management literature. Track it for a week with tally marks; nearly every teacher is surprised by their own number.
4. **The Good Behavior Game,** two or three times a week in grades K through 5. Teams, a short list of behaviors that lose a point, a brief reward for every team that stays under the limit. Free instructions from the PBIS Center and the evidence base in B3. Coaching matters: the replication cohort with less teacher support had smaller effects.
5. **A calm, private, two-step correction.** Step one: restate the expectation to the student, not the room, and move on. Step two: a choice with a consequence you control ("Work here or at the back table; either way the page is due at 10:15"). Only after both steps is it an office matter, and the referral form (`08-data.md`) asks which steps were used.

## Script: the first correction

> "Jada, voice level 1. Thanks." (Turn away. Do not wait for compliance with eye contact; give her ten seconds to save face.)

## Handout

One page: the five routines, the 5:1 tally sheet, and the Good Behavior Game one-page rules.

## When it is not working

- A teacher with referral counts far above the building average is not a discipline problem; they need the coaching visit, not a memo. Pair them with the teacher whose counts are lowest for two observation swaps.
- If referrals cluster in one period, the problem is the schedule or the task, not the students. Fix the transition.


CHAPTER: Module 3: The office: what happens instead of the paddle

**Time:** three hours to build the ladder; posted in every room. **Audience:** principal, assistant principal, anyone who receives referrals.

## What this module replaces

"Three licks or three days." The ladder is a published sequence of responses that every administrator uses the same way, so that students, teachers and parents can predict what happens, and so that the school stops choosing between the paddle and suspension.

## The evidence

- School-wide PBIS, which is this ladder plus the routines in Module 2 plus the data in Module 8, reduced suspensions and office referrals in a five-year randomized trial across 37 schools; children in PBIS schools were 33 percent less likely to be referred (`../evidence.md` B1).
- Consistency across administrators is the fidelity component the trials measured. A ladder used by one administrator and not another is not the intervention.
- The risk of swapping paddling for suspension is real and undocumented in the research (B9). The ladder is designed so that suspension is the last rung, not the second.

## The ladder

Post it. Every rung names who does it, how long it takes, and what the student does next.

| Rung | For | Response | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First minor referral | Re-teach the expectation, five-minute reset in the office, back to class with a note home | Any administrator or the counselor |
| 2 | Repeated minor | Reflection sheet (what happened, who was affected, what I will do next time), a repair task from Module 4, parent call by the student with the administrator present | Administrator |
| 3 | Third minor in a month, or first major (fighting, property, defiance that stopped class) | Same-day parent meeting, restorative conversation with the person affected (Module 4), loss of a privilege tied to the behavior, check-in/check-out for two weeks (Module 5) | Administrator plus teacher |
| 4 | Repeated major | Behavior plan (Module 5), in-school placement with work, daily check-in, weekly parent contact | Leadership team |
| 5 | Safety threat: weapons, serious assault, drugs | Out-of-school suspension per code, with a re-entry meeting on the first day back and a plan from Module 5 | Principal |

Rules that make it work:

- **Nobody skips rungs** except for rung 5 offenses. A teacher who wants rung 3 for a rung 1 behavior gets a conversation, not a suspension.
- **Every rung ends with a return to class and a repair,** because the lesson is that the student belongs here and owes something, not that they are gone.
- **Every rung is logged** on the referral form with the rung number (Module 8). The monthly review looks at whether the rungs are being used as written.
- **The ladder is in the handbook and in the parent letter** (Module 7), in plain language.

## Script: rung 2, the parent call

> "Mrs. Ortiz, this is Devon. I'm in Mr. Hale's office. I want to tell you what happened today." (Student explains. Administrator adds only facts.) "The plan is that I will [repair task] and Mr. Hale will check with Ms. Bell on Friday. Is there anything you want Mr. Hale to know?"

## When it is not working

- If the office is full at rung 1, the classroom routines are not in place; go back to Module 2 rather than adding rungs.
- If one administrator's students climb the ladder faster than another's, that is the fidelity problem the research warns about. The leadership team reviews it monthly by name and rung, and the fix is coaching, not policy.


CHAPTER: Module 4: Repair: restorative conversations that work in five minutes

**Time:** one half-day. **Audience:** all staff.

## What this module replaces

The idea that a consequence is over when the pain stops. A paddled student owes nothing afterward. A student who has had a repair conversation owes something specific to someone specific and usually does it.

## The evidence, honestly

Restorative practices cut days lost to suspension by 36 percent against 18 percent in control schools in a 44-school randomized trial in Pittsburgh and narrowed racial disparities; the same trial found academic outcomes worsened in grades 6 to 8, no effect for students with IEPs, and no drop in arrests. A second randomized trial in 13 Maine middle schools found no significant effects at all (`../evidence.md` B2). So this module is deliberately small: a five-minute conversation script inside the ladder in Module 3, not a whole-school restorative program with circles in every class. Schools with the staff and two years of coaching can go further; this is the version any school can run on Monday.

## The practice: the five questions

Asked by whoever is handling the rung, first to the student who did the harm, then to the person affected, in a quiet place, standing if that helps.

1. What happened?
2. What were you thinking at the time?
3. Who has been affected, and how?
4. What do you need to do to make it right?
5. What will you do differently next time?

The answer to question 4 is the repair task, and it goes on the referral form. Good repair tasks are specific, short, and connected to the harm: clean what was damaged, write or say the apology to the person, help the teacher set up the next day, replace the time taken from the class. Bad repair tasks are generic (an essay on respect) or humiliating (a public apology in front of the class).

## Script

> "Tell me what happened. Just what happened, not why yet." (Listen.) "What were you thinking right then?" (Listen.) "Who else got affected by that? How?" (If the student says "nobody," name one person and ask again.) "So what do you need to do to make it right with Ms. Bell?" (Agree on one thing, with a day.) "Next time you feel that, what will you do instead?"

Two minutes with the person affected: what they need to see happen, and whether they are willing to hear the apology. They can say no.

## Handout

The five questions on a card for every adult's lanyard.

## When it is not working

- A student who cannot answer question 3 is the student Module 5 is for. Log it.
- If repair tasks are not being completed, the follow-up on the referral form is missing; the administrator who assigned it checks on the day named.
- If staff report that "it doesn't change behavior," they are right in the sense the trials found: the change in behavior comes from Modules 2 and 3. This module changes what the consequence means.


CHAPTER: Module 5: The 5 percent: students with repeated referrals

**Time:** three hours, then a weekly 20-minute team meeting. **Audience:** leadership team, counselor if any, the referring teachers.

## What this module replaces

Paddling the same students over and over. Every veteran teacher knows who those students were, which is the plainest evidence that the paddle did not work on them. This module is the plan for the three to five percent of students who generate most referrals.

## The evidence

- Check-In/Check-Out, a daily two-minute morning and afternoon contact with one adult plus a point card carried between classes, is the most studied Tier 2 practice; a systematic review calls its evidence base mixed but sufficient on some outcomes, mostly from single-case designs (`../evidence.md` B4). It is cheap, so its modest evidence is enough to make it the first thing to try.
- The Good Behavior Game's long-term effects were concentrated in the boys who were most aggressive in first grade (B3): universal practice reaches this group most.
- Collaborative and Proactive Solutions (Ross Greene) has randomized clinical evidence for oppositional children equal to parent training, but no whole-school trial (B7). Use its core idea, that the student lacks a skill rather than the will, as the frame for the plan meeting.
- The federal Office of Special Education Programs' 2016 letter states that when a student with an IEP has behavior that impedes learning, the IEP team must consider positive behavioral interventions (B10). Module 6 covers this.

## The practice

**Step 1: Identify.** Any student with three referrals in a month, or two majors, goes on the list. The referral log (Module 8) produces it automatically.

**Step 2: Check-In/Check-Out for two weeks.** Pick one adult the student does not dislike. Two minutes at arrival (card, goal for the day, one encouraging sentence), teacher marks the card each period on the posted expectations, two minutes at dismissal (points, praise for what went right, card home). Weekly graph on the wall of the office.

**Step 3: If the graph is flat after two weeks, hold the plan meeting.** Thirty minutes, student present for part of it, parent invited. Three questions: what is the behavior exactly (when, where, with whom); what does the student get or avoid by it; what skill would they need to not do it. Then one skill to teach, one adult to teach it, one change to the environment (seat, schedule, hallway route), one way the student can ask for a break without a scene, and one thing the parent will do. Written on one page.

**Step 4: Review weekly.** Twenty minutes, the whole list, the graphs. Plans that are working end after four good weeks; plans that are not change one element.

## Handout

The point card template, the one-page plan template, the weekly graph.

## When it is not working

- If the list is more than 10 percent of enrollment, the problem is universal (Modules 2 and 3), not individual.
- If the same student is on the list all year, the question is whether an evaluation for special education services is owed (Module 6) or whether the student needs something the school does not have; the counselor or the district's mental health referral path is the next step, and the plan says so.


CHAPTER: Module 6: Students with disabilities and trauma histories

**Time:** two hours. **Audience:** all staff.

## What this module replaces

The exemption that many paddling policies already carry for students with IEPs, which staff learn as "you can't paddle those kids," without learning what to do instead. Four states now prohibit corporal punishment of students with disabilities by law (`../../facts/claims/law-disability-exemptions-by-state.md`), and the federal data show why: in 2017-18, students with IDEA disabilities were 16.5 percent of students struck and 13.2 percent of enrollment (`crdc-disability-share.md`).

## The evidence, honestly

- A federal letter states that an IEP team must consider, and when necessary include, positive behavioral interventions and supports when a child's behavior impedes learning (`../evidence.md` B10, OSEP 2016). That is a legal obligation, not a suggestion.
- Whole-school trauma-informed programs had no rigorous outcome evidence as of a 2019 Campbell review that found no eligible studies (B6). So this module does not sell a program. It teaches three facts and two habits that are consistent with the practices in Modules 2 through 5, which do have evidence.

## Three facts for every adult

1. **A student in a threat state cannot process instructions.** Cuartas et al. found spanked children's brains respond to threat cues more strongly (A5). Whatever the cause, a student who is escalated needs time and distance before words, which is what the two-step correction in Module 2 and the reset rung in Module 3 provide.
2. **Behavior that looks like defiance is often a skill gap:** language processing, transitions, sensory load, or a home where the adult's raised voice meant a hit. The Module 5 plan meeting asks what skill is missing for a reason.
3. **Restraint and seclusion are not the alternative to paddling.** Idaho's 2023 law banned corporal punishment and limited restraint and seclusion in the same bill (`../../strategy/case-studies/idaho-2023.md`). Physical intervention is for imminent danger only, by trained staff, and is logged and reported to parents the same day.

## Two habits

- **Ask before assuming.** For any student on the Module 5 list, check whether an IEP or 504 plan exists and what it says about behavior. If none exists and the pattern persists, the referral for evaluation is the school's obligation.
- **Predictability is the intervention.** Posted schedules, warnings before transitions, a known adult, a known place to go. Every one of these is free.

## Handout

One page: what an IEP behavior plan must include, the school's restraint and seclusion rules, and the referral path for evaluation.

## When it is not working

If a student with an IEP is climbing the ladder, the IEP team meets, not the discipline office. That is the law and it is also what works.


CHAPTER: Module 7: Parents: the letter, the meeting, the opt-out, the ally

**Time:** one hour. **Audience:** administrators and office staff.

## What this module replaces

The paddle as the parent's proxy. In many paddling communities, parents asked for it, and some will be angry that it is gone. This module is how to keep them.

## The evidence

Parents who understand the ladder use it. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide's fourth recommendation is to draw on colleagues and families (`../evidence.md` B10). The persuasion research in `../../strategy/playbooks/persuasion.md` applies to parents as it does to board members: their own values, a story, one number, a small ask.

## The practice

**The letter** goes home before the policy takes effect. One page. What is changing and when; the ladder, in plain language; what the school is asking parents to do (answer the rung 2 call, come to the rung 3 meeting); and the sentence that matters: "Nothing in this policy changes how you raise your child at home. It changes what school employees do."

**The meeting.** A single evening session, 45 minutes, principal and two teachers. Show the district's own numbers. Say what the school was afraid of and what the plan is. Take every question. The parents who wanted paddling are asking whether the school will still keep order; the answer is the ladder and Module 8's published numbers, not the research.

**The opt-out, while the old policy is still in force.** Any parent may file a written refusal today (`../../templates/letter-principal-opt-out.md`). The school honors every one without exception and keeps a count. That count is a number the board sees.

**The ally.** The parents who file refusals, and the parents who show up to the meeting, are the parents who will speak at the board when the policy comes up for a vote. Keep their names, with permission.

## Script: the parent who wanted paddling

> "I hear you. You want your son to know there are consequences. So do we. Here is exactly what happens now when he does that. And here is what I will ask you to do, which is answer the phone when he calls you from my office. You will hear from us more, not less."

## When it is not working

A parent who insists on paddling can do so at home, where the law allows it, and the school does not argue that. What the school does not do is hit their child for them.


CHAPTER: Module 8: Data: the referral log, the monthly review, the public report

**Time:** one hour to set up, 20 minutes a week. **Audience:** leadership team.

## What this module replaces

Not knowing. Most paddling districts cannot say how many students were struck last year, for what, or whether it was the same students. The federal count is self-reported and, in the Department of Education's words, likely underreported (`../../facts/claims/crdc-undercount-caveat.md`).

## The evidence

Data-based decision making by a school team is a fidelity component of every PBIS trial (`../evidence.md` B1) and the Center on PBIS publishes a free team problem-solving protocol (B10, TIPS). There is no US study of what happened to referrals and suspensions after a district ended corporal punishment (B9). A district that keeps this log before and after its switch and publishes it will have produced the first one.

## The practice

**The referral form**, one page or one screen: student, date, time, location, behavior (from a fixed list of ten), what the teacher did first (the two steps from Module 2), the rung used (Module 3), the repair task and its due date (Module 4), parent contacted (yes, how). No narrative required.

**The log** is a spreadsheet. Free templates exist; a Google Sheet works. Five columns produce everything the team needs: by behavior, by location, by time of day, by teacher, by student.

**The weekly 20 minutes.** Top three behaviors, top three locations, top three times, the Module 5 list. One decision each week about the environment: move the supervision, change the transition, re-teach an expectation in one grade.

**The monthly review.** Referrals per 100 students this month versus the same month last year; suspensions and days lost; rung distribution (is the ladder being used as written); by race, sex and disability status (the same categories the federal collection uses), because disparities that are not measured are not fixed. The Center on PBIS data guide on disproportionality is free (B10).

**The public report.** Once a semester, on the district website: the counts above by school, with no student-level detail. Boards that publish their numbers are trusted; boards that do not are asked for them by records request (`../../templates/public-records-request.md`).

## Handout

The referral form, the log template, the monthly review one-pager.

## When it is not working

- Referrals rise in the first month after the switch in many schools. That is staff referring what they used to handle with a threat, and it is the expected shape of the curve. It comes down in month two if the ladder is used consistently. Say this out loud before the switch so nobody panics.
- If the log is not being filled in, the form is too long. Cut it.

## Slide notes

There is no slide deck and there should not be a long one. Each module's practice section fits on three slides: what it replaces, the practice, the script. The handouts are the takeaway. If a slide has a citation, it comes from `../evidence.md` with the section letter.

## Ground rules for the facilitator

1. Never argue the research with a hostile room. Ask Module 0's second question and wait.
2. Never say the old practice was abuse. Many people in the room administered it in good faith; some were paddled and love the teachers who did it. Say it did not work and this does.
3. Name the fear. "You are worried the building will be chaos. Here is what we will publish every month so you can see whether it is."
4. Give people the script, not the theory. Teachers want to know what to say at 8:05 on the first day.
5. Do the routines in the training. Run the Good Behavior Game with the staff. Practice the five questions in pairs. Nobody learns a script by reading it.

## Adapting for a very small school

A school with one administrator and no counselor runs the ladder with the principal and one lead teacher per grade band sharing rungs 1 and 2. Check-In/Check-Out can be done by the secretary, the custodian, or a coach; the research does not require a counselor, it requires a consistent adult.

## Part A. Harm: what the research shows

### A1. Meta-analysis of parental spanking outcomes — VERIFIED

**Gershoff, E. T., & Grogan-Kaylor, A. (2016).** Spanking and child outcomes: Old controversies and new meta-analyses. *Journal of Family Psychology, 30*(4), 453–469. https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000191 (PMC7992110)

- 75 studies, 111 unique effect sizes, 160,927 children. 17 outcomes analyzed; **13 of 17 significantly associated with spanking, all in the direction of harm**; none in the direction of benefit.
- Effect sizes (Cohen's *d*, positive = worse outcome with spanking), from the paper's results table:

| Outcome | k | d | 95% CI | Significant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate defiance | 5 | .14 | −.19 to .47 | no |
| Low moral internalization | 8 | .38 | .11 to .65 | yes |
| Child aggression | 7 | .37 | .13 to .61 | yes |
| Child antisocial behavior | 9 | .39 | .24 to .53 | yes |
| Child externalizing problems | 14 | .41 | .32 to .50 | yes |
| Child internalizing problems | 8 | .24 | .13 to .35 | yes |
| Child mental health problems | 10 | .53 | .42 to .64 | yes |
| Child alcohol/substance abuse | 3 | .09 | −.11 to .29 | no |
| Negative parent–child relationship | 5 | .51 | .36 to .66 | yes |
| Impaired cognitive ability | 8 | .17 | .01 to .32 | yes |
| Low self-esteem | 3 | .15 | .04 to .26 | yes |
| Low self-regulation | 3 | .30 | −.07 to .67 | no |
| Victim of physical abuse | 8 | .64 | .39 to 1.74 | yes |
| Adult antisocial behavior | 3 | .36 | .06 to .65 | yes |
| Adult mental health problems | 8 | .24 | .09 to .40 | yes |
| Adult alcohol/substance abuse | 4 | .13 | −.08 to .35 | no |
| Adult support for physical punishment | 5 | .38 | .15 to .61 | yes |

- Effect sizes for spanking and for physical abuse did not differ substantially in direction; results did not vary substantially by study design.
- Caveat for the training: this is about *parental* spanking, and the effects are correlational (meta-analysis of mostly non-experimental studies). Use it for the "physical punishment in general" claim, and use A3/A6/A7 for school-specific claims.

### A2. Prevalence and disparities in US public schools — VERIFIED

**Gershoff, E. T., & Font, S. A. (2016).** Corporal punishment in U.S. public schools: Prevalence, disparities in use, and status in state and federal policy. *Social Policy Report, 30*(1), 1–26. https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2379-3988.2016.tb00086.x (PMC5766273)

- Data: US Department of Education Office for Civil Rights, Civil Rights Data Collection, 2011–12 school year.
- **163,333 students** received corporal punishment in 2011–12; 19 states permitted it at the time (AL, AR, AZ, CO, FL, GA, ID, IN, KS, KY, LA, MO, MS, NC, OK, SC, TN, TX, WY).
- **Race:** in Alabama and Mississippi, Black students were at least 51% more likely than white students to be corporally punished in over half of school districts; in one-fifth of districts in those two states, Black students were more than 5 times as likely (709 districts had calculable ratios).
- **Disability:** students with disabilities were more than 50% more likely to be corporally punished in 67% of Alabama districts and 46% of Mississippi districts (1,189 districts with calculable ratios).
- **Gender:** boys were substantially more likely to be paddled than girls in more than three-quarters of districts in Alabama (74%), Arkansas (75%) and Mississippi (78%).
- The paper also documents that CP is concentrated in a small number of Southern states and in specific districts within them.

### A3. School corporal punishment in global perspective — VERIFIED

**Gershoff, E. T. (2017).** School corporal punishment in global perspective: Prevalence, outcomes, and efforts at intervention. *Psychology, Health & Medicine, 22*(sup1), 224–239. https://doi.org/10.1080/13548506.2016.1271955 (PMC5560991)

- At the time of writing, school CP was legally prohibited in 128 countries and allowed in 69 (35%); the US, Australia and South Korea were the only industrialized outliers.
- Reviews the evidence: "There is in fact no evidence that school corporal punishment enhances or promotes children's learning in the classroom." Cites cross-national Young Lives data (Ogando Portela & Pells, 2015) in which school CP at age 8 predicted lower math scores at age 12 in Peru and Vietnam and lower vocabulary in Peru, and in no country predicted better performance.
- Repeats the SAM 2003 estimate: "between 10,000 and 20,000 students required medical attention as a result of school corporal punishment each year in the U.S." (see A9 below).
- Notes documented injuries (bruising, broken bones) from US court cases (Ingraham v. Wright, 1977; Garcia v. Miera, 1987) and that the UN has recorded child deaths from school CP in other countries.

### A4. Physical punishment, cognition and school adjustment — VERIFIED

**Font, S. A., & Cage, J. (2018).** Dimensions of physical punishment and their associations with children's cognitive performance and school adjustment. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 75*, 29–40. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2017.06.008 (PMID 28743493)

- 658 children aged 8–14 at baseline, followed 3 years; punishment coded as mild corporal punishment, harsh corporal punishment, or physical abuse; both caregiver and child reports.
- After controls, **only physical abuse** was significantly associated with declines in cognitive performance. However, **all forms of physical punishment**, including mild CP, were associated with **declines in school engagement**; harsh CP was associated with increased peer isolation.
- Honest reading for the training: mild CP did not measurably lower test performance in this sample, but it did predict disengagement from school. Do not overclaim cognitive harm from this study.

### A5. Neural response to threat — VERIFIED

**Cuartas, J., Weissman, D. G., Sheridan, M. A., Lengua, L., & McLaughlin, K. A. (2021).** Corporal punishment and elevated neural response to threat in children. *Child Development, 92*(3), 821–832. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13565

- fMRI; 147 children (40 who had been spanked, 107 who had not; mean age 11.6).
- Spanked children showed greater activation to fearful vs. neutral faces in multiple regions of medial and lateral prefrontal cortex (dorsal anterior cingulate, dorsomedial PFC, bilateral frontal poles, left middle frontal gyrus).
- Authors' interpretation: spanking may alter neural threat responses in a way similar to more severe maltreatment. Caveats: cross-sectional, modest n, no school-CP sample.

### A6. School CP and later achievement/adjustment (propensity-matched) — VERIFIED, with a citation correction

**Gershoff, E. T., Sattler, K. M. P., & Holden, G. W. (2019).** School corporal punishment and its associations with achievement and adjustment. *Journal of Applied Developmental Psychology, 63*, 1–8. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appdev.2019.05.004 (PMC7405866)

- **Correction:** this paper is in *Journal of Applied Developmental Psychology*, not *Psychology, Public Policy, and Law*. No 2019 Gershoff/Sattler/Holden paper in PPPL was found in Europe PMC or OpenAlex. Cite the JADP paper.
- Anonymous online survey of 800+ emerging adults (18–23) in the 19 states where school CP was legal; 16% reported having experienced school CP. Propensity-score matching on covariates.
- School CP was associated with **lower high-school GPA, higher current depressive symptoms, and greater intention to spank their own children**.
- It is a state-level *sample*, not a state-level disparity analysis; the disparity findings are in A2.

### A7. Meta-analysis specific to school corporal punishment — VERIFIED

**Visser, L. N., van der Put, C. E., & Assink, M. (2022).** The association between school corporal punishment and child developmental outcomes: A meta-analytic review. *Children, 9*(3), 383. https://doi.org/10.3390/children9030383 (PMC8946887)

- Three-level meta-analyses. School CP associated with **externalizing behavior r = 0.27** (21 studies, 120 effect sizes, N = 67,400), **internalizing behavior r = 0.16** (14 studies, N = 39,917), and **lower school performance r = −0.11** (20 studies, 47 effect sizes, N = 977,367); all p < .001.
- This is the best single citation for "school CP is linked to worse behavior and lower achievement." Correlational.

### A8. Systematic review of school CP correlates — VERIFIED

**Heekes, S.-L., Kruger, C. B., Lester, S. N., & Ward, C. L. (2022).** A systematic review of corporal punishment in schools: Global prevalence and correlates. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 23*(1), 52–72. https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020925787

- 53 quantitative studies (1980–2017), all cross-sectional. School CP is associated with physical, academic, mental-health and behavioral problems; boys, Black students (US) and students exposed to violence at home are most at risk. Schools employing a mental-health professional and training staff in safety procedures were less likely to use CP. Notes that CP persists in many places despite bans, which matters for implementation planning.

### A9. Injury estimate: 10,000–20,000 students need medical attention yearly — REPORTED (secondary-verified)

**Greydanus, D. E., Pratt, H. D., Spates, C. R., Blake-Dreher, A. E., Greydanus-Gearhart, M. A., & Patel, D. R.; Society for Adolescent Medicine Ad Hoc Corporal Punishment Committee. (2003).** Corporal punishment in schools: Position paper of the Society for Adolescent Medicine. *Journal of Adolescent Health, 32*(5), 385–393. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1054-139X(03)00042-9 (PMID 12729988)

- Bibliographic record VERIFIED (Europe PMC). The 10,000–20,000 figure was VERIFIED as quoted in Gershoff (2017, PMC5560991), which attributes it to this paper. The SAM full text itself could not be opened (publisher paywall). Treat the number as an early-2000s estimate, not a measured count; there is no federal injury surveillance for school CP.

### A10. State-level ecological comparison — VERIFIED

**Arcus, D. (2002).** School shooting fatalities and school corporal punishment: A look at the states. *Aggressive Behavior, 28*(3), 173–183. https://doi.org/10.1002/ab.90020

- Across 50 states, after adjusting for poverty and religious affiliation, states permitting CP had significantly more school shooting deaths (OR 2.04 for states allowing CP; 1.77 for states restricting it). Ecological; do not present as causal.

### A11. The "states that allow CP have lower ACT scores" claim — NOT VERIFIED

- I could not locate a primary source. The claim has circulated in advocacy fact sheets attributed to the Center for Effective Discipline (stophitting.com, now returning 403) and nospank.net (now a parked domain). The 2008 Human Rights Watch/ACLU report *A Violent Education* does **not** make an ACT claim. No peer-reviewed study comparing state ACT averages by CP legality was found in Europe PMC or OpenAlex.
- Even if the correlation is real, it is a state-level ecological comparison confounded by region, poverty, and the share of students taking the ACT. **Recommendation: do not use it.** Use Visser et al. 2022 (r = −0.11 for school performance) and Gershoff, Sattler & Holden 2019 (lower HS GPA) instead.

### A12. Does banning CP change behavior? What the evidence can and cannot say

- **Elgar, F. J., Donnelly, P. D., Michaelson, V., Gariépy, G., Riehm, K. E., Walsh, S. D., & Pickett, W. (2018).** Corporal punishment bans and physical fighting in adolescents: An ecological study of 88 countries. *BMJ Open, 8*(9), e021616. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-021616 — VERIFIED. 403,604 adolescents. Compared with 20 countries with no ban, the 30 countries with full bans (school and home) had 69% the rate of frequent fighting in males and 42% in females; 38 countries with school-only bans had less fighting among females (56% of the no-ban rate). Authors state causality is unclear.
- **Zolotor, A. J., & Puzia, M. E. (2010).** Bans against corporal punishment: A systematic review of the laws, changes in attitudes and behaviours. *Child Abuse Review, 19*(4), 229–247. https://doi.org/10.1002/car.1131 — VERIFIED. 24 countries; bans are associated with declining support for and use of CP; direction of causality unclear. Mostly about parental bans.
- **Gopalan, M., et al. (2024).** Historical patterns and trends in racial/ethnic disproportionality in school discipline in the United States. *Socius*. https://doi.org/10.1177/23780231241285105 — REPORTED (seen only as an OpenAlex record). Documents that US school CP rates have fallen steadily since the early 1980s as states banned it.
- **Gap, stated plainly:** I found **no US natural-experiment or difference-in-differences study** showing what happened to misconduct, suspension, or achievement after a state or district abolished CP. The 2023 AAP statement and Gershoff (2017) argue from the absence of any evidence that CP improves behavior, plus the harm evidence above, not from post-ban outcome studies. The training should say: "No study has found that ending corporal punishment increases misbehavior; no study has found that using it improves behavior." Both halves are accurate; a stronger causal claim is not supported.

## Part B. Alternatives: what works, with honest limits

### B1. School-Wide Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (SWPBIS) — strongest evidence base

- **Bradshaw, C. P., Mitchell, M. M., & Leaf, P. J. (2010).** Examining the effects of schoolwide positive behavioral interventions and supports on student outcomes: Results from a randomized controlled effectiveness trial in elementary schools. *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions, 12*(3), 133–148. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300709334798 — VERIFIED (abstract). 5-year group-RCT, 37 Maryland elementary schools. Schools trained in SWPBIS and implementing with fidelity had **significant reductions in suspensions and office discipline referrals** vs. controls. (Abstract reports direction and significance; per-outcome effect sizes are in the paper's tables, not opened here.)
- **Bradshaw, C. P., Waasdorp, T. E., & Leaf, P. J. (2012).** Effects of school-wide positive behavioral interventions and supports on child behavior problems. *Pediatrics, 130*(5), e1136–e1145. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2012-0243 — VERIFIED (abstract). Same trial; 12,344 children. Significant improvements in teacher-rated behavior problems, concentration, social-emotional functioning and prosocial behavior; children in SWPBIS schools were **33% less likely to receive an office discipline referral**; effects strongest for children first exposed in kindergarten.
- **Horner, R. H., Sugai, G., Smolkowski, K., Eber, L., Nakasato, J., Todd, A. W., & Esperanza, J. (2009).** A randomized, wait-list controlled effectiveness trial assessing school-wide positive behavior support in elementary schools. *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions, 11*(3), 133–144. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300709332067 — VERIFIED (abstract). Hawaii and Illinois; training produced higher implementation fidelity, improved perceived safety and improved third-grade reading; ODRs were low but the authors note the discipline data lacked experimental control.
- **Limits:** effects are modest to moderate and depend on fidelity; most RCT evidence is elementary; SWPBIS is a framework, not a packaged curriculum, so "adopting PBIS" without the tiered systems, data use and coaching does not reproduce these results.

### B2. Restorative practices — promising for suspensions, mixed elsewhere

- **Augustine, C. H., Engberg, J., Grimm, G. E., Lee, E., Wang, E. L., Christianson, K., & Joseph, A. A. (2018).** *Can restorative practices improve school climate and curb suspensions? An evaluation of the impact of restorative practices in a mid-sized urban school district.* RAND Corporation, RR-2840-DOJ. https://doi.org/10.7249/RR2840 — VERIFIED (full PDF). Two-year RCT in Pittsburgh Public Schools (2015–17), 44 schools (22 treatment).
  - Days lost to suspension fell **36% in treatment schools vs. 18% in control schools**; share of students suspended was 13% in treatment vs. 15% in control (16% baseline). Disparities by race and income narrowed. Teachers rated school climate higher.
  - **Negative and null findings:** academic outcomes did not improve overall and **worsened in grades 6–8** in treatment schools; no suspension reduction in middle grades, for male students, for students with IEPs, or for violence/weapons incidents; **no reduction in arrests**. Surveyed staff did not believe student behavior itself had improved.
- **Acosta, J., Chinman, M., Ebener, P., Malone, P. S., Phillips, A., & Wilks, A. (2019).** Evaluation of a whole-school change intervention: Findings from a two-year cluster-randomized trial of the Restorative Practices Intervention. *Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 48*(5), 876–890. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-019-01013-2 — VERIFIED (abstract). 13 Maine middle schools, 2,771 students. **"The intervention did not yield significant changes in the treatment schools."** Students' self-reported *experience* of restorative practices did predict better climate, connectedness and less cyberbullying, which is a dose/implementation signal, not an intent-to-treat effect.
- **Honest summary for the training:** restorative practices can cut suspensions and disparities in elementary grades; two RCTs show no reliable effect on student behavior or achievement, and one shows academic harm in middle school. Present as a complement to PBIS, not a stand-alone replacement, and plan for two-plus years of coaching.

### B3. Good Behavior Game (GBG) — strongest long-term evidence for a classroom practice

- **Kellam, S. G., Rebok, G. W., Ialongo, N., & Mayer, L. S. (1994).** The course and malleability of aggressive behavior from early first grade into middle school: Results of a developmental epidemiologically-based preventive trial. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 35*(2), 259–281. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1994.tb01161.x — VERIFIED (abstract). ~1,000 Baltimore first graders, classroom-randomized; reduced sixth-grade teacher-rated aggression among boys who were more aggressive in first grade.
- **Kellam, S. G., Brown, C. H., Poduska, J. M., Ialongo, N. S., Wang, W., Toyinbo, P., Petras, H., Ford, C., Windham, A., & Wilcox, H. C. (2008).** Effects of a universal classroom behavior management program in first and second grades on young adult behavioral, psychiatric, and social outcomes. *Drug and Alcohol Dependence, 95*(Suppl 1), S5–S28. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2008.01.004 (PMC2512256) — VERIFIED (abstract). 19 Baltimore schools; by ages 19–21, significantly reduced drug and alcohol abuse/dependence, regular smoking and antisocial personality disorder, concentrated among males who were aggressive/disruptive in first grade. A replication cohort with less teacher mentoring showed smaller effects.
- **Kellam, S. G., Mackenzie, A. C. L., Brown, C. H., Poduska, J. M., Wang, W., Petras, H., & Wilcox, H. C. (2011).** The Good Behavior Game and the future of prevention and treatment. *Addiction Science & Clinical Practice, 6*(1), 73–84. (PMC3188824) — VERIFIED (abstract). Summarizes lower rates of substance-use disorders, regular smoking, antisocial personality disorder, delinquency/incarceration for violent crime, suicidal ideation and school-service use among GBG students.
- **Kellam, S. G., et al. (2014).** The impact of the Good Behavior Game... on high-risk sexual behaviors and drug abuse and dependence disorders into young adulthood. *Prevention Science, 15*(Suppl 1), S6–S18. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-012-0296-z — VERIFIED (abstract).
- **Limits:** the long-term effects are strongest for initially aggressive boys; the replication with weaker teacher coaching produced weaker effects, so coaching is part of the intervention.

### B4. Check-In/Check-Out (CICO) — Tier 2, moderate evidence

- **Hawken, L. S., MacLeod, K. S., & Rawlings, L. (2007).** Effects of the Behavior Education Program (BEP) on office discipline referrals of elementary school students. *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions, 9*(2), 94–101. https://doi.org/10.1177/10983007070090020601 — VERIFIED (record/abstract). 12 at-risk elementary students; ODRs fell for most participants when implemented with fidelity; single-case design.
- **Maggin, D. M., Zurheide, J., Pickett, K. C., & Baillie, S. J. (2015).** A systematic evidence review of the Check-In/Check-Out program for reducing student challenging behaviors. *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions, 17*(4), 197–208. https://doi.org/10.1177/1098300715573630 — VERIFIED (abstract). Applied WWC standards to single-case and group studies; **mixed**: enough evidence to call CICO evidence-based on some outcomes, no documented effects in others. Best described as "promising, with a mostly single-case evidence base."

### B5. Classroom management training

- **Korpershoek, H., Harms, T., de Boer, H., van Kuijk, M., & Doolaard, S. (2016).** A meta-analysis of the effects of classroom management strategies and classroom management programs on students' academic, behavioral, emotional, and motivational outcomes. *Review of Educational Research, 86*(3), 643–680. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315626799 — VERIFIED (abstract). 54 controlled studies (2003–2013), primary grades; **average g = 0.22**, significant for academic, behavioral and social-emotional outcomes but not motivation; programs focused on social-emotional development contributed most; academic outcomes benefited from teacher-focused programs.
- **Incredible Years Teacher Classroom Management (IY TCM):**
  - **Webster-Stratton, C., Reid, M. J., & Stoolmiller, M. (2008).** Preventing conduct problems and improving school readiness: Evaluation of the Incredible Years Teacher and Child Training Programs in high-risk schools. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 49*(5), 471–488. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2007.01861.x — VERIFIED (abstract). Matched-pair school RCT, 153 teachers, 1,768 Head Start/K/1 students; intervention teachers used more positive management and students showed more social competence, better emotional self-regulation and fewer conduct problems (blinded observation). Note the teacher program was bundled with the Dinosaur School child curriculum.
  - **Reinke, W. M., Herman, K. C., & Dong, N. (2018).** The Incredible Years Teacher Classroom Management Program: Outcomes from a group randomized trial. *Prevention Science, 19*(8), 1043–1054. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-018-0932-3 — VERIFIED (abstract). 105 teachers, 1,817 K–3 students; **small effects: emotional dysregulation d = −0.14, prosocial behavior d = 0.13, social competence d = 0.13**, larger for students starting with lower competence. Present IY TCM as effective but small in average effect.
- **CHAMPS (Sprick / Safe & Civil Schools):** the only empirical evaluation located in OpenAlex is a 2016 Walden University dissertation (Evans, qualitative, 7 teachers). **No peer-reviewed RCT found.** Treat CHAMPS as a practitioner-designed package that operationalizes the same principles as the WWC guide, not as an independently proven program.
- **Kounin, J. S. (1970).** *Discipline and Group Management in Classrooms.* Holt, Rinehart & Winston. — Existence and year VERIFIED (Open Library); publisher REPORTED. Observational study that introduced "withitness," overlapping, momentum and smoothness; foundational but pre-experimental. Cite as origin of concepts, not as outcome evidence.

### B6. Trauma-informed approaches — evidence is thin

- **Maynard, B. R., Farina, A., Dell, N. A., & Kelly, M. S. (2019).** Effects of trauma-informed approaches in schools: A systematic review. *Campbell Systematic Reviews, 15*(1–2), e1018. https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.1018 (PMC8356508) — VERIFIED (full text). Searches through June 2017; **"no studies met inclusion criteria."** There was no rigorous outcome evidence for whole-school trauma-informed approaches as of 2017. Some newer studies exist but none rise to the RCT level cited here. The training should describe trauma-informed practice as a values/knowledge frame that is consistent with the evidence-based practices above, not as an evidence-based program in itself.

### B7. Collaborative & Proactive Solutions (Ross Greene, Lives in the Balance) — clinical evidence, little school evidence

- **Greene, R. W., Ablon, J. S., Goring, J. C., et al. (2004).** Effectiveness of collaborative problem solving in affectively dysregulated children with oppositional-defiant disorder: Initial findings. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 72*(6), 1157–1164. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.72.6.1157 — VERIFIED (abstract). 47 children, outpatient; CPS equivalent or superior to parent training at post and 4-month follow-up.
- **Ollendick, T. H., Greene, R. W., Austin, K. E., et al. (2016).** Parent Management Training and Collaborative & Proactive Solutions: A randomized control trial for oppositional youth. *Journal of Clinical Child & Adolescent Psychology, 45*(5), 591–604. https://doi.org/10.1080/15374416.2015.1004681 (PMC4564364) — VERIFIED (abstract). 134 youth with ODD; CPS and PMT both beat waitlist and did not differ; ~50% diagnosis-free after either treatment.
- **Schaubman, A., Stetson, E., & Plog, A. (2011).** Reducing teacher stress by implementing collaborative problem solving in a school setting. *School Social Work Journal, 35*(2), 72–93. — Record VERIFIED (OpenAlex/ERIC); design not opened. This is the school-based study Lives in the Balance cites; it is small and pre-post, not an RCT. (The livesinthebalance.org research page returned a server error during this check.)
- **Honest summary:** CPS is an evidence-based *clinical* treatment for ODD; its whole-school effects have not been tested in an RCT. Present it as a promising skill set for Tier 3 problem-solving with individual students.

### B8. MTSS

- MTSS is the umbrella framework (academic RTI plus PBIS). Its behavioral evidence *is* the SWPBIS evidence in B1; there is no separate RCT of "MTSS" as such. Cite B1 and the PBIS.org practice guide "Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS) in the Classroom" (B10). Do not claim MTSS has its own outcome evidence.

### B9. What happened where CP was dropped — the honest answer

- No study located quantifies suspensions, ODRs or achievement before and after a US district or state abolished CP (see A12). Two contextual facts are verified: (1) districts that stop paddling still have exclusionary discipline available, so a rise in suspensions is plausible in the absence of an alternative system, which is exactly why the training pairs abolition with PBIS/GBG; (2) the Pittsburgh RCT shows a district can reduce suspensions by adopting an alternative system, and the Maryland RCTs show the same for PBIS. Frame the risk as "swapping paddling for suspension" and cite B1/B2 as the remedy, without asserting any measured post-ban suspension effect.
