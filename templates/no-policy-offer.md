# When a district says it has no written policy

Some districts answer a policy request with "we don't have one." In a permitting state that means
the practice runs on custom: nothing prohibits it, nothing governs it, and the district has reported
using it. That is the opening, not the end of the conversation. The reply below offers the district
a way to close the gap on its own terms: a model policy, the replacement curriculum in `training/`,
and coaching through the change, all free, framed around the goals the district already publishes
(safety, attendance, achievement, a climate parents trust). Send it as the reply to their "no".

Do not call it a grant unless money is attached. A program is what we can promise today.

```
Subject: Re: Request for your district's corporal punishment policy

Thank you for the straight answer. A district without a written policy on this is more common than
people think, and it puts the district in a hard spot: the practice is on the federal record for
[DISTRICT] ([N] students in 2023-24), and there is nothing on paper that says who may do it, when,
how, or how a parent can decline.

- a replacement discipline curriculum for staff (ten short modules, evidence-cited, built for
  small schools), which the district may adopt in whole or in part;
- implementation coaching for the superintendent and principals through the first year, including
  the board presentation and the parent letter.

There is no cost and no obligation. The aim is to align what the district does with what it says
it wants for its students — [QUOTE THE DISTRICT'S OWN MISSION OR GOAL LINE HERE].
```
