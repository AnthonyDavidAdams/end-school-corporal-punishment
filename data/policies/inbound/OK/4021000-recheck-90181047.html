Oklahoma Cost Accounting System (OCAS)

Oklahoma Cost Accounting SysteM

OCAS is the State Department of Education mandated coding system for all Oklahoma Public Schools. SDE provides an Annual Manual each year as well as periodic validation rules that determine allowable combinations between the various components of OCAS (such as Function combinations with Program and Subject codes).  The manual and more can be found on the OSDE website.

1. State Aid Formula
What is State Aid?

State Aid is the primary method Oklahoma uses to distribute state education funding to public school districts. The formula is designed to provide additional funding based on student enrollment, student needs, and a district's local wealth.

The Formula in Plain English
Think of it as a four-step process.
Step 1: Count Students (Average Daily Membership)

Everything begins with Average Daily Membership (ADM) - the number of students enrolled and attending school. More students generally mean more funding.

Step 2: Apply Student Weights

Not every student generates the same amount of funding. Students with greater educational needs receive additional "weights."

Imagine your school has:

  • 100 students in Grades 3-12
  • 20 Economically Disadvantaged students
  • 8 English Learners
  • 12 Gifted students

Those students generate:

  • Base membership = 100
  • Economic Disadvantage = +5.0
  • EL = +2.0
  • Gifted = +4.08

Weighted Membership = 111.08

These factors are combined to create a district's Weighted Average Daily Membership (WADM), which is the foundation of the formula.

Step 3: Calculate Foundation Aid
The district's Weighted ADM is multiplied by the state's annually established base support factor to determine its Foundation Aid before local wealth is considered.

Conceptually:

Weighted Students X State Base Amount = Foundation Aid

Step 4: Subtract Local Wealth
Oklahoma is an equalization state.

Districts with:

  • higher property values
  • more ad valorem tax collections
  • local revenues

receive less State Aid, while districts with lower local tax capacity receive more State Aid.

"Chargeable" local revenues are deducted from the foundation calculation as part of the formula.

Additional Formula Components

State Aid also includes:

  • Transportation Aid (hasn't changed since 1984) Reimbursed for distances over 1.5 miles
  • Salary Incentive Aid

These are calculated separately and added to the district's allocation (not much $ for MPS).

Principals influence district funding every day.
1. Enrollment Matters

Every student matters. Losing students reduces revenue. Keeping students enrolled supports district funding.

2. Attendance Matters
While the formula is based on membership rather than daily attendance alone, attendance data affects numerous state and federal funding streams, accountability measures, and can influence future funding calculations. Improving attendance is still financially important for the district.
 
3. Accurate Student Coding Matters

Funding depends on correctly identifying eligible students.

Examples include:

  • English Learners
  • Economically Disadvantaged
  • Gifted
  • Special Education

Incorrect coding can result in lost revenue or audit findings. This is WHY Free / Reduced forms are so very important!

4. Documentation Matters

If it isn't documented:

  • the district may lose funding
  • auditors may disallow expenditures
  • grant funds may have to be repaid

Every principal affects district finances by:

  • Recruiting and retaining students
  • Improving attendance
  • Ensuring accurate enrollment and student coding
  • Supporting timely data reporting
  • Protecting district resources through good stewardship
2. Local ad valorem taxes

Ad valorem taxes are local property taxes assessed on real estate, personal property, and certain public service property located within a school district. These taxes provide a significant local funding source for Oklahoma public schools. (64% for MPS)

How are they used?

Local property taxes help fund three primary purposes:

  • General Fund (fund 11) - Supports day-to-day operations such as teacher salaries, instructional supplies, utilities, and other operating expenses.
  • Building Fund (fund 21) - Pays for repairs, maintenance, insurance, and certain facility improvements.
  • Sinking Fund - (fund 41) - Used exclusively to repay voter-approved bond debt for capital projects such as new schools, major renovations, and large equipment purchases.
Why do principals need to understand ad valorem taxes?
  • Property values vary significantly from district to district.
  • Districts with higher property values generate more local revenue, while districts with lower property values receive more State Aid through Oklahoma's equalization funding formula.
  • Local ad valorem revenue is one of the key factors used when calculating a district's State Aid allocation.

Local property taxes are the community's investment in its schools. They provide funding for daily operations, facility maintenance, and voter-approved capital improvements. Oklahoma's State Aid Formula then adjusts state funding to help balance differences in local property wealth among districts.

3. Federal program

Federal program dollars are restricted funds provided by the U.S. Department of Education and other federal agencies to support specific student populations and educational initiatives. Unlike State Aid or local property taxes, these funds must be spent only for the purposes authorized by the grant and require detailed documentation and reporting.

Common Federal Programs
 
Program Purpose
Title I, Part A Provides supplemental instructional support for students in schools with higher concentrations of poverty.
IDEA (Special Education) Supports special education services for students with disabilities ages 3-21.
Title II, Part A Funds professional development and leadership development for teachers and principals.
Title III Supports English Learners (ELs) and immigrant students through language instruction and related services.
Title IV, Part A Supports student well-being, school safety, mental health, technology, and well-rounded educational opportunities.
Title V (REAP) Provides flexible funding for eligible rural school districts.
 
Key Characteristics
  • Restricted funds - May only be used for approved purposes.
  • Supplement, not supplant - Federal funds must add to existing educational services rather than replace state or local funding.
  • Annual applications and budgets - Districts must apply for and manage each grant.
  • Extensive documentation - Purchases, payroll, inventories, and expenditures are subject to federal monitoring and audit.
  • Time-limited - Funds generally must be obligated and spent within specified grant periods.
As a principal, you play a critical role in ensuring federal funds are used appropriately by:
  • Following approved grant budgets.
  • Obtaining approval before making purchases.
  • Maintaining required documentation.
  • Ensuring federally purchased equipment is inventoried and used for its intended purpose.
  • Working with the district's Federal Programs office before changing how grant-funded resources are used.

Federal grants provide valuable resources for students with specific needs, but they come with strict spending rules. Every expenditure must support the grant's purpose, be properly documented, and comply with federal requirements. Using federal funds incorrectly can require the district to repay the money and can result in audit findings.

4. Activity Funds

School Activity Funds are locally controlled funds generated by students, parents, and the community to support school-sponsored cocurricular and extracurricular activities. Unlike General Fund dollars, Activity Funds are not State Aid or tax revenue and must be used for the benefit of the specific student organization or activity that generated the money. Under Oklahoma law, the local board of education has control over all activity funds, and each activity must maintain its own subaccount.

Common Sources of Revenue
  • Student fundraisers
  • Athletic gate receipts
  • Concession sales
  • Club dues
  • Yearbook sales
  • Donations
  • Vending and school store proceeds
  • Ticket sales for performances and events
What Can Activity Funds Be Used For?

Activity Funds may be used to support student activities such as:

  • Club and organization expenses
  • Student travel
  • Competition entry fees
  • Athletic equipment and supplies
  • Student recognition and awards
  • Student event supplies
  • Yearbooks, performances, and extracurricular programs

Expenditures should directly benefit students and be consistent with board policy and the purpose of the activity that generated the funds.

Key Responsibilities

The principal serves as the custodian of the school's Activity Fund and is responsible for ensuring:

  • All money is deposited promptly.
  • Accurate records are maintained.
  • Purchases follow district policies.
  • Funds are spent only for approved purposes.
  • Activity accounts are reconciled regularly.
  • Internal controls protect school assets.

Activity Funds are audited annually and are subject to Oklahoma law and district policy.

Activity Funds are student-generated dollars held in trust by the school. Principals are responsible for safeguarding these funds and ensuring they are used legally, ethically, and for the direct benefit of students.

5. Bond Fund

Bond Funds consist of proceeds from voter-approved general obligation bonds. These funds are used to finance major capital improvements and long-term assets that benefit students and the community over many years. Bond proceeds must be spent only on the projects approved by the voters in the bond election.

Common Uses of Bond Funds
Bond funds are commonly used for:
  • New school construction
  • Classroom additions and renovations
  • Roofs, HVAC, windows, and major facility improvements
  • School buses
  • Furniture and equipment
  • Instructional technology
  • Safety and security improvements
  • Land acquisition and site improvements

Each bond issue is accounted for separately and expenditures are tracked by the specific voter-approved projects.

What Bond Funds Cannot Be Used For

Bond funds cannot be used for the district's normal operating expenses, including:

  • Teacher and staff salaries
  • Utilities
  • General classroom supplies
  • Routine maintenance
  • Daily operating costs

These expenses must generally be paid from the General Fund or other appropriate funding sources.

Key Responsibilities

Because bond funds come from voter approval, districts must ensure that:

  • Expenditures match the approved bond proposition.
  • Purchases comply with state purchasing laws and district policies.
  • Projects are properly documented and accounted for.
  • Funds are used only for authorized capital purposes.

Bond Funds are for long-term investments—not day-to-day operations. These dollars allow districts to build, renovate, equip, and secure schools while preserving General Fund dollars for educating students. Every bond dollar must be spent exactly as approved by the voters.

6. Building Fund

The Building Fund is a locally funded operating fund that supports the maintenance, repair, operation, and improvement of school facilities and grounds. It is primarily funded through a voter-approved building fund levy of up to five (5) mills, along with certain state grants, donations designated for capital improvements, and interest earnings.

Common Uses of Building Fund Dollars

Building Fund dollars may be used for:

  • Building repairs and maintenance
  • HVAC, plumbing, electrical, and roofing repairs
  • Grounds maintenance and landscaping
  • Utilities (electricity, natural gas, water)
  • Property and casualty insurance
  • Security systems and security personnel
  • Furniture, equipment, and computer software
  • School buses and certain maintenance equipment
  • Telecommunications and internet services
How is the Building Fund Different?

Unlike the General Fund, which primarily supports instruction and daily educational operations, the Building Fund focuses on keeping district facilities safe, functional, and operational.

Unlike Bond Funds, Building Fund dollars do not require a bond election and are generally used for ongoing facility needs rather than major construction projects.

Key Responsibilities

Principals help protect Building Fund resources by:

  • Reporting maintenance needs promptly.
  • Prioritizing repairs that protect students and district assets.
  • Working with Maintenance and Finance before purchasing facility-related items.
  • Distinguishing between routine maintenance (Building Fund) and major capital projects (Bond Fund).

The Building Fund keeps our schools operating every day. It pays for maintaining, repairing, protecting, and operating our facilities so teachers can teach and students can learn in a safe environment.

PRINCIPAL RESPONSIBILITIES

As a building principal, you are entrusted with managing public resources in a way that supports student achievement while ensuring compliance with district policies, state law, and sound financial practices. Every financial decision should reflect good stewardship, accountability, and transparency.

Key Responsibilities
Budget Management
  • Plan and prioritize school needs.
  • Monitor expenditures throughout the year.
  • Stay within approved budgets.
  • Work with the Finance Office when adjustments are needed.
Purchasing
  • Follow district purchasing policies.
  • Obtain purchase orders before committing district funds.
  • Ensure purchases are reasonable, necessary, and benefit students.
  • Verify goods and services are received before payment.
Activity Funds
  • Serve as the custodian of school activity funds.
  • Ensure fundraising proceeds are properly collected, deposited, and accounted for.
  • Approve expenditures that directly benefit the student organization generating the funds.
  • Maintain strong internal controls over cash handling.
Stewardship of District Assets
  • Safeguard equipment, technology, furniture, and other district property.
  • Ensure inventories remain accurate.
  • Report damaged, lost, or stolen property promptly.
Compliance

WHY DO PURCHASE ORDERS EXIST?

In Oklahoma school districts, purchase orders (POs) are much more than an internal accounting tool—they are a legal control required by state law to ensure a district does not spend money it has not been legally appropriated or authorized to spend.

Why Purchase Orders Exist

1. To prevent spending money that is not available

Before a purchase order can be issued, the district's encumbrance clerk must verify that sufficient appropriated funds exist in the proper budget account.

Oklahoma law states:

"Prior to the issuance of a purchase order, the encumbrance clerk must first determine that the encumbrance will not exceed the balance of the appropriation to be charged."

This process "encumbers" (reserves) the funds before the district becomes legally obligated to pay a vendor.

2. To ensure purchases are authorized before they occur

Oklahoma law is very direct:

"Before any purchase is completed, a purchase order or encumbrance must be issued." This means a school employee should not order goods or services first and request a PO later. The purchase order is intended to come before the district incurs the obligation.

3. To maintain budgetary control

Purchase orders allow the district to:

  • reserve budgeted funds
  • prevent overspending
  • know how much money remains available
  • accurately project year-end balances
  • ensure expenditures stay within board-approved appropriations

Without encumbrances, districts could unknowingly spend the same funds multiple times.

4. To provide accountability and transparency

The purchase order process creates a documented audit trail showing:

  • who requested the purchase
  • who approved it
  • which budget account is paying
  • that funding was available
  • when the goods or services were received
  • what invoice was ultimately paid

State law also requires:

  • itemized invoices
  • proof of receipt
  • filing of the invoice with the encumbrance records before payment is made.

5. To protect taxpayers and the school board

The purchase order system protects:

  • taxpayers from unauthorized spending,
  • principals and employees from accidentally violating purchasing laws,
  • board members by ensuring expenditures are within legally adopted appropriations.

The district cannot legally create debt beyond available appropriations.

Oklahoma Statutes

The primary statute is:

70 O.S. § 5-135 – System of Accounting

Important provisions include:

  • Board of education must use a prescribed accounting system for purchases.
  • Authorized purchasers must be designated.
  • Encumbrance clerk verifies available appropriations.
  • Encumbrances are recorded before obligations occur.
  • Purchase orders are approved by the Board of Education and reflected in the board minutes.
  • Before any purchase is completed, a purchase order or encumbrance must be issued.

Another related statute is:

70 O.S. § 5-187

This statute requires claims against the prior fiscal year to be supported by an approved purchase order and submitted within the statutory deadline, reinforcing that the PO is the legal basis for payment.

A purchase order is the school district's legal permission to spend public money. It confirms that:

  • the purchase has been approved,
  • money is available,
  • the expense fits the approved budget,
  • and the district can legally pay the vendor.

Without a purchase order issued before the purchase, the district may not have legal authority to pay the invoice.

RECEIVING (Amazon Example)

Verifying receipt of Amazon purchases (or any other purchase) before submitting an invoice for payment is one of the most important internal controls for our school district. Unlike many traditional vendors, Amazon orders may arrive in multiple shipments, include substitutions, contain damaged items, or have items that are backordered or canceled. Paying an invoice before confirming receipt increases the risk of paying for items the district never actually received.

Why Verification of Receipt Is Critical
1. The District Should Only Pay for Goods Actually Received (Legal Requirement)

School districts are stewards of taxpayer dollars. Before payment is made, the employee receiving the order should verify:

  • Every item listed on the invoice was delivered.
  • Quantities match what was ordered.
  • The correct products were received.
  • Items are undamaged and usable.

This protects public funds and ensures the district only pays for what it actually received.

2. Amazon Frequently Ships Orders in Multiple Deliveries

Unlike many vendors, Amazon often:

  • splits one order into several shipments,
  • delays individual items,
  • substitutes products,
  • cancels unavailable items.

If an invoice is submitted after only the first box arrives, the district may inadvertently pay for products that have not yet been delivered. (Illegal!)

3. It Protects Employees

When an employee signs or approves an invoice, they are essentially certifying:

"The district has received these goods and they are acceptable for payment." (met the legal requirement!!)

If items are missing after payment has been made, recovering funds becomes much more difficult.

4. It Reduces Fraud and Mistakes

Receipt verification helps detect:

  • missing packages,
  • incorrect shipments,
  • duplicate shipments,
  • unauthorized purchases,
  • stolen deliveries,
  • vendor billing errors.

These errors are much easier to correct before payment than afterward.

5. It Meets Oklahoma Internal Control Requirements

Oklahoma's school accounting system requires documentation supporting every payment.

70 O.S. § 5-135 requires that claims for payment be supported by proper documentation, including invoices and evidence that the district incurred the obligation through the purchase order process. Payment should occur only after the district has confirmed that the goods or services have been received and the claim is valid.

In addition, Oklahoma school accounting procedures prescribed by the State Department of Education require districts to maintain adequate internal controls over receiving and payment processing to safeguard public funds.

6. It Helps Auditors

During annual financial audits, auditors commonly ask questions such as:

  • Was there an approved purchase order?
  • Was the purchase authorized?
  • Was the item actually received?
  • Is there evidence of receipt?
  • Does the invoice match the purchase order?

Documented receipt verification provides evidence that the district followed proper purchasing procedures.

INVOICES

Following invoicing rules is not simply a matter of district policy—it is a legal responsibility.

Oklahoma school districts spend public funds, and every employee involved in purchasing, receiving, or approving invoices plays a role in ensuring those funds are spent lawfully, accurately, and transparently. Failure to follow established invoicing procedures can result in audit findings, delayed payments, budget overruns, and in some cases, violations of Oklahoma law.

Why Following Invoicing Rules Is So Important
1. Public Money Requires Public Accountability

Every dollar spent by an Oklahoma school district comes from taxpayers or public funding sources. Employees have a fiduciary responsibility to ensure that every payment is:

  • properly authorized,
  • supported by documentation,
  • for a legitimate school purpose,
  • and accurately charged to the correct budget.

The purchase order and invoice process creates the documentation needed to demonstrate that public funds were spent appropriately.

2. Oklahoma Law Requires Strong Internal Controls

70 O.S. § 5-135(C) requires every board of education to establish and administer adequate business procedures and controls governing purchases and the delivery of goods and services.

These procedures must identify authorized purchasers, require acceptable invoices, and establish a method for determining the employee who verifies receipt of each purchase.

This means invoicing procedures are not optional—they are part of the district's legally required internal control system.

3. No Invoice Should Be Paid Without Proper Documentation

Under 70 O.S. § 5-135(E):

  • A purchase order or encumbrance must be issued before a purchase is completed.
  • After satisfactory receipt of the goods or services, the designated employee must certify the bill for payment.
  • The encumbrance clerk may not pay a claim unless it is supported by an itemized invoice describing the items purchased, quantities, unit prices, and total cost.
  • The invoice must be maintained as part of the district's official accounting records.

These requirements protect the district from paying unsupported or inaccurate claims.

4. Verifying Receipt Prevents Paying for Items Never Received

The employee approving an invoice is confirming that:

  • the order was actually received,
  • it matches the purchase order,
  • quantities are correct,
  • items are undamaged,
  • and the district received what it is paying for.

This is especially important with vendors such as Amazon, where orders are frequently shipped in multiple packages, partially fulfilled, substituted, or canceled.

5. Proper Invoicing Protects Employees

When an employee approves an invoice, they are certifying that the district's payment is justified.

Following the required procedures protects employees from being associated with:

  • duplicate payments,
  • unauthorized purchases,
  • missing inventory,
  • vendor billing errors,
  • audit exceptions,
  • questioned costs.

Proper documentation demonstrates that the employee exercised due diligence.

6. It Helps Prevent Fraud and Abuse
Strong invoice procedures are one of the most effective internal controls against:
  • fictitious invoices,
  • duplicate invoices,
  • unauthorized purchases,
  • personal purchases,
  • overbilling,
  • theft of district property,
  • vendor fraud.
Requiring multiple documents—purchase order, receiving verification, and itemized invoice—reduces the opportunity for mistakes or misconduct.
 
7. It Supports Accurate Budget Management

Invoices are used to:

  • release purchase order encumbrances,
  • accurately record expenditures,
  • maintain current budget balances,
  • prepare financial reports,
  • support state reporting through the Oklahoma Cost Accounting System (OCAS).

Incomplete or inaccurate invoices can result in incorrect financial records and budget information. Oklahoma law requires districts to operate under OCAS, and failure to do so can affect state aid payments.

8. It Ensures a Successful Audit

Each year, independent auditors review whether school districts followed Oklahoma purchasing laws and internal controls.

Auditors routinely verify that:

  • a purchase order existed before the purchase,
  • goods or services were received,
  • the invoice matches the purchase order,
  • payment was properly approved,
  • documentation is complete.

Following invoicing procedures provides the evidence needed to demonstrate compliance.

9. Late or Improper Invoices Can Result in Claims Being Denied

Under 70 O.S. § 5-187, invoices and required documentation for goods or services delivered under an approved purchase order must be submitted within the statutory deadlines established by the district. Claims that are not timely submitted may become void and forever barred. Every employee who purchases, receives, or approves invoices is helping protect the district's financial integrity.

Before approving an invoice, always verify that:

  • ✓ A purchase order was issued before the purchase.
  • ✓ The goods or services have been received in satisfactory condition.
  • ✓ The invoice matches the purchase order.
  • ✓ Quantities and prices are correct.
  • ✓ Required documentation is attached.
  • ✓ The purchase serves a legitimate school purpose.

Following invoicing rules protects everyone. It protects taxpayers by ensuring public funds are spent legally, protects the district by maintaining accurate financial records, and protects employees by documenting that purchases were authorized, received, and properly approved.

Oklahoma law requires these procedures because every public dollar must be traceable, supported, and accountable. A properly documented invoice is not just paperwork—it is the legal evidence that the district has fulfilled its responsibility to safeguard public funds.

Module 5 - Activity Funds

In Oklahoma, activity funds are public funds held in trust by the school district (with limited exceptions for certain student charitable fundraisers). Because these funds often involve cash collections, Oklahoma law and State Department of Education rules require strong internal controls over collection, safeguarding, depositing, and documenting every dollar received.

1. The Board of Education Controls All Activity Funds

Under 70 O.S. § 5-129, the local board of education exercises control over all activity funds generated by student activities and other approved revenue sources. These monies belong in the school activity fund and may only be spent for the purpose for which they were collected. The board must approve activity fund subaccounts, fundraising activities, and the authorized purposes for expenditures.

What this means for employees

Employees and sponsors are custodians of public money-they do not own or control these funds personally. Every dollar collected belongs to the school district until it is spent according to board-approved purposes.

2. Cash Must Be Deposited Promptly

Oklahoma law establishes a specific timeline for deposits.

70 O.S. § 5-129 requires:

  • Deposits must be made by the end of the next business day.
  • If daily collections total less than $100, the district may accumulate those collections.
  • Even when daily receipts are under $100, deposits must be made at least once each week.
Why this matters

The longer cash remains in a classroom, office, or desk drawer:

  • the greater the opportunity for theft,
  • the greater the chance of loss,
  • the more difficult it becomes to prove how much money was actually collected.

Prompt deposits are one of the strongest fraud prevention controls.

3. Gate Receipts Must Be Carefully Accounted For

Admissions to athletic events, plays, concerts, carnivals, dances, and similar activities are specifically authorized activity fund revenue under 70 O.S. § 5-129(B).

Good internal control practices require:

  • prenumbered tickets whenever practical,
  • beginning and ending ticket inventories,
  • reconciliation of tickets sold to cash collected,
  • dual counting of cash,
  • documented cash count sheets,
  • prompt deposit into the activity fund.

These procedures create an audit trail that demonstrates all admission revenue was deposited.

4. Do Not Hold Cash Overnight Unless District Procedures Allow It

While Oklahoma statutes do not expressly state "cash shall never be held overnight," the statutory requirement for next-business-day deposits reflects the Legislature's expectation that cash be safeguarded and deposited promptly.

If money must remain overnight because the event ends after banking hours:

  • it should be secured immediately,
  • stored in a locked safe or approved secure location,
  • access should be restricted,
  • deposited on the next business day.

Cash should never be:

  • left in classrooms,
  • stored in desk drawers,
  • taken home by employees,
  • kept in personal vehicles.

These practices expose both the employee and the district to unnecessary risk.

Module 6 - Bond Funds vs General Fund
BOND FUNDS

School bond proceeds in Oklahoma are restricted capital funds. They cannot be used for the district's normal operating expenses. Instead, they are intended to finance long-term capital improvements that voters approved in the bond election. This separation is reflected in Oklahoma law, which excludes bond proceeds from the General Fund and limits them to capital purposes.

What Bond Funds CAN Be Used For

Bond proceeds may generally be used for long-term capital assets and improvements, including:

  • Constructing new school buildings
  • Purchasing land or improving school sites
  • Remodeling or renovating existing buildings
  • Major repairs that extend the useful life of facilities
  • Classroom additions
  • Roof replacements
  • HVAC replacements
  • Electrical, plumbing, and mechanical system upgrades
  • Security improvements
  • Technology infrastructure that becomes part of the facility
  • Permanent furniture, fixtures, and equipment
  • Athletic facilities and other capital improvements approved by the voters
  • Costs directly related to the bond project, including certain professional services (architectural, engineering, surveying, testing, etc.) and authorized bond issuance costs

The language approved by voters in the bond election also controls how proceeds may be spent. Districts should ensure expenditures are consistent with both state law and the specific purposes described in the bond proposition.

What Bond Funds CANNOT Be Used For

Bond proceeds generally may not be used for current operating expenses, including:

  • Teacher or support staff salaries
  • Employee benefits
  • Substitute teachers
  • Classroom instructional supplies that are consumable
  • Office supplies
  • Utilities
  • Fuel
  • Routine maintenance
  • Food service expenses
  • Student travel
  • Professional development
  • Athletic officials or game expenses
  • Regular transportation costs
  • Any other recurring operational expense

These costs belong in the General Fund or another legally authorized operating fund—not the Bond Fund. Oklahoma law defines the General Fund as the district's current expense fund and specifically excludes bond proceeds from it.

Why Oklahoma Law Separates Bond Funds from General Funds

70 O.S. § 1-117 defines the General Fund as the district's current expense fund and expressly states that it does not include monies derived from the sale of bonds. The statute further provides that General Fund expenditures are noncapital in nature, reinforcing that bond proceeds are reserved for capital purposes.

This separation ensures taxpayers receive exactly what they approved when voting for a bond issue.

Special Rules

A few additional requirements apply to bond funds:

  • Every expenditure must be consistent with the purposes approved by voters.
  • All bond transactions must be recorded through the Oklahoma Cost Accounting System (OCAS).
  • After all approved bond projects (or like projects) are completed, any remaining surplus bond proceeds are transferred to the district's Sinking Fund by board action recorded in the board minutes.

Oklahoma Law Defines Capital vs. Noncapital

70 O.S. § 1-117 provides the legal distinction.

Capital Expenditures

Capital expenditures generally involve:

  • acquiring fixed assets,
  • constructing facilities,
  • adding to existing facilities,
  • purchasing land,
  • major structural remodeling,
  • significant improvements that extend the useful life or capacity of a building.

Noncapital Expenditures

Noncapital expenditures include:

  • maintenance,
  • repairs,
  • replacement of property,
  • furniture,
  • equipment,
  • maintenance of grounds,
  • routine upkeep,
  • repairs that do not alter roof structures or load-bearing walls.

This distinction is critical because it determines which fund may legally pay the expense.

Limited Exceptions

Oklahoma law permits a district to use General Fund dollars for certain capital expenditures only in limited circumstances, including:

  • rebuilding after a fire or natural disaster when insurance and other funding are insufficient,
  • certain State Board of Education-approved circumstances after a failed bond election,
  • limited classroom construction or remodeling in qualifying low-property-wealth districts that meet specific statutory conditions.

These exceptions are narrow and require compliance with the conditions set out in 70 O.S. § 1-117(F), (K), and (L).

What is a budget (in terms of school finance)?

A plan for the utilization of revenues for expenditures over a specific time period (i.e., a fiscal year --July 1 to June 30).  This is not the “Estimate of Needs” that is prepared each year by the auditor.  Rather, the budget is a much more detailed “financial plan” for the district.

Is a budget required?

Yes, required by Oklahoma Statutes (Title 70 O.S. Sec 5- 134.1) school districts must “...adopt a budget for the current fiscal year.  The budget shall include functional categories as defined by the Oklahoma Cost Accounting System (OCAS).”

What is an "Estimate of Needs?"

Oklahoma Statutes (Title 68 O.S. Sec. 3002) require the Board of Education, prior to Sept. 1 of each year, to “...make a written itemized statement of estimated needs (expenditures) and probable income from all sources including ad valorem taxes for the current fiscal year ...” The Estimate of Needs (EoN) includes all appropriated funds of the district: General Fund, Building Fund, Child Nutrition Fund, Bond Funds, and Sinking Fund.

Is the EoN basically the operating budget of the District?

No.  The Estimate of Needs establishes a maximum amount that the Board of Education can approve for its various budgets. By law, the district’s operational budget cannot exceed the “total appropriation” (maximum allowable amount) established by the Estimate of Needs.

Does the EoN include all revenue sources estimated to be received by the District during the fiscal year?

Yes. However, by law, the Estimate of Needs can only include 90% of the projected tax collections from Ad Valorem (property tax) revenues for the General Fund and Building Fund, as 10% is required to be excluded as a “delinquency reserve”. Normally, school districts estimate between 93% and 97% of ad valorem collection for their operational budgets. All other revenue sources are based on historical collection rates or official allocation notices for state and or official allocation notices for state and federal grant programs.

How is an Operational Budget prepared?

A brief summary of the major steps to preparing an operational budget are:

  1.  Estimate the total amount of revenue to be received during the fiscal year based on historical collection rates, trends, or allocation notices. 
  2.  In addition to the revenue estimates, the fund balance (carryover) from the previous fiscal year is included to determine the total funds available.
  3.  Estimate total expenditures by category. These major categories, listed below, should be based on historical costs, new or increased costs, new programs, and other factors. 

a) Payroll/benefit costs (this is the largest and most important estimate.) 
b) Fixed costs (insurance, utilities, etc.) 
c) Operational budgets (schools, depts.) 
d) Other curriculum/instructional and related costs (textbooks, testing, professional development, etc.)

The total operational budget must not exceed the “total appropriation” amount authorized by the Estimate of Needs. Also, the operational budget should be prepared  with proper planning so total expenditures are less than total funds available (revenues plus fund balance) to ensure that a fund balance amount will be carried over at the end of the fiscal year.

What is "fund balance?"

Fund balance (a.k.a. carryover or surplus) is the “unencumbered” or “available” balance of funds at the end of a fiscal year that has not been reserved to pay for any goods/services which will be received after June 30.

Why is a fund balance necessary?

A fund balance is necessary for the following reasons:

  1. To help districts fund payroll and other costs during the first half of the school year when expenditures are more than revenue collections because there is no State Aid payment received in July, and the majority of ad valorem tax revenue is not received until January and April. (NOTE: State Aid payments are received equally over a period of 11 months - August through June. State Aid is not paid to the district in a “lump sum” at the beginning of the fiscal year.)
  2. To meet unanticipated expenses. 
  3. To help fund future budgets, if planned expenditures exceed anticipated revenues. The percentage of fund balance that can be carried into the next year is limited by state law. The penalty for excess carryover is a reduction to state aid the next year.

What are the school district's revenue sources?

  • Local (39%) - Ad valorem taxes, interest earnings, tuitions, rentals and reimbursements
  • Intermediate (5.5%) - County 4-mill taxes, resale property fund, apportionment taxes 
  • State (45%) - Gross production taxes, REA taxes, motor vehicle taxes, school land earnings, tax stamps, State Aid, state categorical grants
  • Federal/Other (10.5%) - Federal grant programs, activity fund reimbursements, etc.

What is "state aid?"

State Aid represents the funds that are appropriated by the State Legislature for school districts and distributed by the State Dept. of Education through the “State Aid Formula”. State Aid is based primarily on student counts, with allowances made for various student characteristics represented as grade and categorical weights. State Aid uses the higher of the current or two previous years’ student counts. Thus, if a district’s student count increases, the State Aid is adjusted in the current year. 
If a district’s student count decreases, the State Aid does not decrease for two years. The State Aid calculated using these student counts is then reduced for local revenue collections by subtracting “chargeables.”

What are "chargeables?"

“Chargeables” are a district’s local revenue sources that are included in its annual State Aid calculation. The revenue sources are: Ad valorem property taxes, motor vehicle collections, gross production taxes, school land earnings, county 4-mill taxes and rural electric association taxes. The amount of these funds collected by a district (current year for ad valorem & previous year for others) are “charged” or subtracted from the district’s State Aid since these funds are collected locally. As these revenue sources rise (like ad valorem taxes), the district’s “chargeables” also increase, meaning less State Aid for the district.

What is the "General Fund?"

The General Fund provides direct and support services for all students enrolled in the district. This includes salaries/benefits, instructional supplies/materials, equipment, utilities, insurance, other costs.

School districts typically spend 80 to 95 percent of the General Fund on salaries and benefits.

What portion of the General Fund is spent on salaries/benefits?

Approximately 92%.

What is the cost for Administrators?

In Oklahoma, administrative costs for schools are subject to certain limits, and the Oklahoma State Department of Education (OSDE) has regulations and guidelines regarding how these costs are calculated and managed, particularly for schools contracting with educational management organizations. 

This is calculated under Title 70 O.S. Sec 18- 124. Districts have percent limitations on administrative costs. If a district exceeds the allowable administrative costs the district’s state aid will be reduced the following year.  Mustang Public Schools is allowed up to 5%, but is currently at ~3.4% .

Can school districts borrow money?

Oklahoma statutes generally prohibit school debt, except for the following three instances:

1. Bond issues

A.  Lease-purchase agreements
B.  Non-payable warrants

2. The most common way that school districts “borrow” money is through the passage of bond issues.

3. State law requires approval of at least 60% of the voters in a bond election before a district can issue bonds.

What can Bond proceeds be spent for?

Bond issue proceeds must be spent for improving or acquiring school sites, constructing, repairing, remodeling and equipping school buildings, acquiring school furniture, fixtures, equipment, uniforms, technology and transportation equipment. No less than 85 percent of all bond issue proceeds must be spent on the list of projects included in the bond resolution and approved by the voters. The remaining 15 percent can be spent on similar-type projects as approved in the bond issue election or can be used to reduce the sinking fund debt. By law, no salaries, benefits or related costs can be paid with bond fund proceeds or interest earnings.

How are Bond issues paid off?

Bond principal and interest are paid from ad valorem tax revenues. Districts collect ad valorem taxes through a tax levy assessed on real property, personal property and public service property and place these collections in the “Sinking Fund.” This tax levy is referred to as the “Sinking Fund mill levy” and is separate from the General Fund and Building Fund tax levies School bonds are limited to a 25 year term by state law, but five to ten year bonds are typical. The Sinking Fund is also used to pay any judgments levied against the school district. By law, no other expenditures can be paid from the Sinking Fund. The Sinking Fund levy changes each year based on the total property valuation in the district and the amount of bond principal and interest paid off during the year.

What is a "Mill Levy," and how is it used to calculate property taxes?

A “mill” is a term that is equivalent to one-tenth (1/10) of one penny ($0.001). The total mill levy for a taxpayer’s property includes all levies of the following entities that can choose to levy a tax.

1. School district

2. Vo-Tech district

3. County / City

4. Hospitals

5. Library

Below is an example of how to calculate the school district’s portion of taxes levied for the calendar year:

EXAMPLE: $100,000 home in a district with a $1,000 homestead exemption. $100,000 X 11% assessment ratio (Oklahoma County) = $11,000 $11,000 - $1,000 exemption = $10,000

a. Total millage levy: 1. General Fund 36.98 mills, Building Fund 5.28 mills, Sinking Fund 24.04 mills, Total levy 66.30 mills.

b. Total school district taxes: $10,000 X 0.06630 (66.30 mills) = $663.00 ($55.25 per month) To determine the total tax bill for the year, the other mill levies (vo-tech, county, etc.) must be included.
