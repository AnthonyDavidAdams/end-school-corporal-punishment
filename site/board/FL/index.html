One sentence.

For Florida school board members: what the law permits, what the research finds, where the protection for staff stops, and the one sentence that ends it.

The four-minute film, Texas edition: the research and the ask are the same everywhere; the Florida figures are below. A Florida edition is in production.

“The use of corporal punishment is prohibited.”

5 Florida boards already use this sentence, in policy 5630. For a district that permits corporal punishment on paper and has not used it in years, adopting the sentence changes nothing on Monday morning except the exposure. In Florida, 2 districts are in exactly that position.

Florida in numbers

467 students were struck in Florida public schools in 2023-24. In 2017-18 it was 1,332: the practice is already leaving. Of the students struck, 83 percent were boys and 28 percent had a disability.

U.S. Department of Education Civil Rights Data Collection.

What the research finds

Across 21 studies and 67,400 children, school corporal punishment is associated with more aggressive and disruptive behavior, more anxiety and depression, and lower school performance. Across 75 studies it did not even reliably stop the behavior in the moment. Every source is at Facts.

What Florida law says

Fla. Stat. § 1003.32(1)(k) (teacher authority per board policy) and § 1002.20(4)(c) (as amended by CS/HB 1255, 2025, ch. 2025-110) permits it. The law's own conditions: Since July 1, 2025 a board that authorizes it must require parental consent, for the school year or before each instance (the statute says 'consent,' not 'written consent'); the board must review the policy at a public meeting every three years or the authorization expires; charters must comply (§ 1002.33(16)(b)17); Principal approval in principle and principal guidelines; another adult present and informed of the reason; written explanation to the parent on request (§ 1003.32(1)(k)); No disability ban. Every immunity statute runs out at the policy's edge: where the policy is not followed, the protection is gone, and whether it was followed is decided afterward, by someone else.

Who already did it

22 of the 54 Florida boards on the public record prohibit it in writing. 5 of them use the sentence above. See every Florida district's own policy line.
