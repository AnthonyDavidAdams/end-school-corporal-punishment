Verified facts

The numbers

  • Black students were 26 percent of students receiving corporal punishment in 2021-22 and 15 percent of enrollment; Black boys alone were 8 percent of enrollment and 20 percent of students struck. (2021-22) source · file
  • Boys were 81 percent of students receiving corporal punishment in 2021-22 while making up 51 percent of enrollment. (2021-22) source · file
  • Students with disabilities served under IDEA were 16.5 percent of students receiving corporal punishment in 2017-18 against 13.2 percent of enrollment; by 2021-22, after Mississippi's 2019 ban on the practice for students with IEPs, IDEA students were 12 percent of those struck against 14 percent of enrollment. (2017-18 and 2021-22) source · file
  • In the 2021-22 school year, 24,534 K-12 public school students received corporal punishment in the United States, according to the federal Civil Rights Data Collection. (2021-22) source · file
  • In the 2023-24 school year, 19,851 K-12 public school students received corporal punishment, computed by this project from the raw Civil Rights Data Collection file released August 31, 2026; the Office for Civil Rights has published no analysis of that release. (2023-24) source · file
  • Students receiving corporal punishment in US public schools fell from 106,055 in 2013-14 to 92,479 in 2015-16, 69,492 in 2017-18, about 19,400 in the pandemic year 2020-21, and 24,534 in 2021-22. (2013-14 to 2021-22) source · file
  • In 2021-22, 948 school districts (5.4 percent of the nation's 17,704) and 2,050 schools (2.1 percent) reported at least one student receiving corporal punishment. (2021-22) source · file
  • Texas, Alabama, Mississippi and Arkansas accounted for 74 percent of all US students receiving corporal punishment in 2021-22 (7,324; 4,083; 3,678; 3,165 of 24,534). (2021-22) source · file
  • The federal count of corporal punishment is self-reported by districts and, in the Department of Education's own words, 'likely underreported'; it counts students, not incidents, and skips virtual schools. (2023-03-24) source · file

States and the law

  • 196 states are party to the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; the United States, which signed it in 1995, is the only UN member state that has not ratified it. (2026-09-09) source · file
  • Seven federal circuits allow a student to sue over excessive corporal punishment under substantive due process ('shocks the conscience'), two under the Fourth Amendment, and the Fifth Circuit alone, covering Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, denies any federal claim where state remedies exist; the Supreme Court has declined to review that rule five times, most recently in January 2024. (2024-01-08) source · file
  • Colorado prohibited corporal punishment in public schools and licensed child care on April 20, 2023 through HB23-1191, which passed the House 48-16 and the Senate 32-0. (2023-04-20) source · file
  • Five states prohibit corporal punishment of students with disabilities while still permitting it for other students: Louisiana (2017), Tennessee (2018, with a parental-permission exception), Arkansas (2019, four categories), Mississippi (2019) and Oklahoma (2025). (2025-05) source · file
  • The Protecting Our Students in Schools Act, which would bar corporal punishment in any school receiving federal funds, was introduced as H.R. 3265 on May 8, 2025 with 21 cosponsors, all Democrats, and referred to the House Education and Workforce Committee; no version since 2010 has had a hearing or markup, and as of September 2026 no Senate companion has been introduced in the 119th Congress despite press releases saying one would be. (2025-05-08) source · file
  • Since July 1, 2025, Florida districts that allow corporal punishment must obtain parental consent, either for the school year or before each instance, under HB 1255 (the statute says 'consent,' not 'written consent'; the second-adult and written-explanation rules predate it in § 1003.32), which passed both chambers unanimously on final votes and was signed May 30, 2025. (2025-07-01) source · file
  • Idaho banned corporal punishment and chemical restraint in public and charter schools on April 3, 2023 through House Bill 281, a restraint-and-seclusion bill carried by a Republican sponsor; it passed the House 45-22 and the Senate 21-12. (2023-04-03) source · file
  • In Ingraham v. Wright (1977), a 5-4 Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not apply to corporal punishment in public schools and that due process does not require a hearing before paddling because state tort and criminal remedies suffice; it expressly left open whether excessive corporal punishment violates substantive due process. (1977-04-19) source · file
  • New Mexico prohibited corporal punishment in schools through HB 172, signed by Republican Governor Susana Martinez on April 6, 2011 after passing the House 36-31 and the Senate 22-17. (2011-04-06) source · file
  • Oklahoma's SB 364, prohibiting corporal punishment of students with disabilities, became law in May 2025 without Governor Kevin Stitt's signature after passing the Senate 31-16 and the House 63-25. (2025-05) source · file
  • Three states now require a parent's affirmative consent before a school may paddle a child: Missouri (written permission, 2022), Louisiana (annual written consent on a state form, 2023) and Florida (consent for the year or per instance, 2025); Kentucky reached the same rule by regulation in 2022 and every district then quit. (2025-07-01) source · file
  • Five states prohibit corporal punishment in private schools as well as public: New Jersey, Iowa, Maryland (HB 185, 2023), New York (Chapter 551, 2023) and Illinois (Public Act 103-0806, effective January 1, 2025). (2025-01-01) source · file
  • South Carolina's only statute on the subject, § 59-63-260, unchanged since 1973, says the governing body of each school district 'may provide corporal punishment for any pupil that it deems just and proper,' with no limits, and no bill to change it was filed in the 2023-24 or 2025-26 sessions. (2026-09-09) source · file
  • As of September 2026, corporal punishment in public schools is legal by statute in 17 states; in two of them, Kentucky and North Carolina, every district has prohibited it by policy, leaving 15 where it is both legal and in use. (2026-09-09) source · file
  • Texas HB 772, which would have banned corporal punishment in public schools, failed on second reading in the House on April 26, 2023 by a reported 58-86; its sponsor, Rep. Alma Allen, has filed the bill every session for 18 years. (2023-04-26) source · file
  • Wyoming's statute does not authorize corporal punishment outright; it grants school staff civil and criminal immunity for 'reasonable corporal discipline' authorized by board policy, and bills to repeal that immunity failed in the House in 2023 (12-47) and failed introduction in 2026 (38-24, short of two-thirds). (2026-02-09) source · file

Campaigns that worked

  • Kentucky recorded zero instances of corporal punishment in the 2023-24 school year after all 171 school districts adopted prohibiting policies by November 2, 2023, driven by a state board regulation rather than a statute. (2023-24) source · file
  • All 115 North Carolina school districts had prohibited corporal punishment by October 2, 2018, when Graham County became the last to vote to end it, although state law (G.S. 115C-390.4) still permits it. (2018-10-02) source · file

The research

  • No study has found that ending corporal punishment increases misbehavior, and no study has found that using it improves behavior; as of September 2026 there is no US natural-experiment study of suspensions or achievement before and after a state or district ban. (2026-09-09) source · file
  • In a 2021 fMRI study of 147 children, those who had been spanked showed greater prefrontal activation to fearful faces, a pattern the authors compared to more severe maltreatment. (2021) source · file
  • Across 88 countries and 403,604 adolescents, countries with full bans on corporal punishment had lower rates of frequent physical fighting (69 percent of the no-ban rate for boys, 42 percent for girls), and countries with school-only bans had less fighting among girls. (2018) source · file
  • In a three-year study of 658 children, only physical abuse predicted declines in cognitive performance, but every form of physical punishment, including mild corporal punishment, predicted declines in school engagement. (2018) source · file
  • A 2016 meta-analysis of 75 studies covering 160,927 children found spanking significantly associated with 13 of 17 outcomes examined, every one in the direction of harm and none in the direction of benefit, including aggression (d = .37), mental health problems (d = .53) and later victimization by physical abuse (d = .64). (2016) source · file
  • In a propensity-matched survey of more than 800 young adults from the 19 states where school corporal punishment was legal, those who had received it in school had lower high-school GPAs, more depressive symptoms, and greater intention to spank their own children. (2019) source · file
  • A 2022 meta-analysis specific to school corporal punishment found it associated with more externalizing behavior (r = .27, 21 studies, 67,400 children), more internalizing problems (r = .16) and lower school performance (r = −.11, 20 studies, 977,367 children). (2022) source · file

What works instead

  • A 2016 meta-analysis of 54 controlled studies found classroom management programs improved students' academic, behavioral and social-emotional outcomes with an average effect of g = 0.22, with programs focused on social-emotional development contributing most. (2016) source · file
  • Free, federally produced guides cover every practice in the training: the What Works Clearinghouse practice guide on reducing elementary behavior problems (2008), the Center on PBIS practice guides, and the Department of Education's 2023 Guiding Principles, which contains a section titled 'Ending the practice of corporal punishment in schools.' (2023-03) source · file
  • Children randomized to classrooms using the Good Behavior Game in first and second grade had, by ages 19 to 21, significantly lower rates of drug and alcohol disorders, regular smoking and antisocial personality disorder, with the largest effects among boys who were most aggressive in first grade. (2008) source · file
  • In a five-year randomized trial across 37 Maryland elementary schools, School-Wide Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports reduced suspensions and office referrals, and children in PBIS schools were 33 percent less likely to receive an office discipline referral. (2010-2012) source · file
  • In a two-year randomized trial in 44 Pittsburgh schools, restorative practices cut days lost to suspension by 36 percent against 18 percent in control schools and narrowed racial disparities, but academic outcomes worsened in grades 6 to 8, arrests did not fall, and a second randomized trial in 13 Maine middle schools found no significant effects. (2018-2019) source · file
  • A 2019 Campbell systematic review of trauma-informed approaches in schools found no studies that met its inclusion criteria; whole-school trauma-informed programs had no rigorous outcome evidence as of 2017. (2019) source · file

The rest of the world

  • The United Kingdom ended corporal punishment in state schools by statute in 1986, New Zealand by statute in 1990, Canada by a 2004 Supreme Court ruling, South Korea by a 2011 national decree after provincial ordinances, and Australia state by state between 1983 and 2019. (2026-09-09) source · file
  • Seventy states have prohibited corporal punishment of children in all settings, including the home. (2026-09) source · file
  • Of the 38 OECD countries, corporal punishment in schools is fully prohibited in 36; the exceptions are the United States and Australia, where Queensland has no statutory ban for non-government schools. (March 2025) source · file
  • Corporal punishment is fully prohibited in schools in 137 of the 199 states and territories tracked by the Global Initiative to End All Corporal Punishment of Children; in 62 it is not. (March 2025) source · file

Who has taken a position

  • The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry's 2012 policy statement does not support corporal punishment as a method of behavior modification and urges its members to advise school boards accordingly. (2012-07-30) source · file
  • The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2018 policy statement on discipline concludes that aversive strategies including all forms of corporal punishment are minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term. (2018-11) source · file
  • In August 2023 the American Academy of Pediatrics published a policy statement recommending that corporal punishment in all school settings be abolished in all states by law and replaced by alternative forms of behavior management. (2023-08-21) source · file
  • The American Federation of Teachers has no resolution opposing school corporal punishment; its only resolution on the subject (1976) intervened in Ingraham v. Wright to protect 'a teacher's right to maintain discipline in the classroom,' though the AFT endorsed the federal Protecting Our Students in Schools Act in 2023. (1976) source · file
  • The American Psychological Association adopted a resolution in February 2019 opposing physical discipline of children by parents, citing the 2016 meta-analysis; it does not address school corporal punishment directly. (2019-02) source · file
  • The National Association of School Psychologists 'strongly opposes the use of corporal punishment in schools and supports ending its use in all schools' (position statement, revised 2021). (2021) source · file
  • National PTA's position statement says it will support efforts to abolish corporal punishment and to develop alternative discipline programs. (1989 (reported)) source · file
  • In November 2016 more than 100 organizations, including the ACLU, APA, AFT, NEA, AAP, NAACP, SPLC and the Children's Defense Fund, signed an open letter organized by the National Women's Law Center calling for an end to corporal punishment in schools. (2016-11-21) source · file

Courts

  • In October 2023 a Texas judge dismissed the assault charge against an Overton ISD principal who had paddled a student hard enough to leave bruising a child-abuse physician called consistent with abuse, because district policy was followed and the mother was present and consenting. (2023-10-25) source · file

POLICY: PROHIBITION OF CORPORAL PUNISHMENT

Policy Number: [NUMBER]
Adopted: [DATE]
[DISTRICT NAME] School District

I. PURPOSE
The Board of Education recognizes that maintaining a safe, supportive learning environment requires effective discipline strategies that do not rely on physical punishment.

II. POLICY STATEMENT
Corporal punishment is prohibited in all schools and programs operated by the [DISTRICT NAME] School District. No employee, volunteer, or contractor shall inflict corporal punishment on any student.

III. DEFINITION
Corporal punishment means the intentional infliction of physical pain upon a student's body as a disciplinary measure. This includes, but is not limited to: paddling, spanking, slapping, or any other form of physical punishment.

IV. EXCEPTIONS
This policy does not prohibit the use of reasonable physical intervention necessary to:
- Protect a student or others from imminent physical harm
- Obtain possession of a weapon or dangerous object
- Protect property from serious damage
- Maintain order when other means have failed

V. ALTERNATIVE DISCIPLINE STRATEGIES
The District shall provide training to all staff on positive behavioral interventions and supports (PBIS), restorative practices, and other evidence-based discipline strategies.

VI. VIOLATIONS
Any employee who violates this policy shall be subject to disciplinary action, up to and including termination of employment.

VII. REPORTING
Any incident involving physical contact with a student for discipline purposes must be documented and reported to the building principal within 24 hours.

Approved by the Board of Education on [DATE].

State-Level Ban

Placeholders are in [BRACKETS]. Facts cited in this template are maintained in facts/claims/; check facts/README.md for the current figures before sending.

AN ACT relating to student discipline in public schools.

BE IT ENACTED BY THE LEGISLATURE OF THE STATE OF [STATE]:

SECTION 1. PROHIBITION OF CORPORAL PUNISHMENT

(a) No person employed by or volunteering for a public school district shall inflict or cause to be inflicted corporal punishment upon a student.

(b) For purposes of this section, "corporal punishment" means the deliberate infliction of physical pain as a means of discipline, including but not limited to:
    (1) Paddling, spanking, or hitting;
    (2) Slapping or striking;
    (3) Any other form of physical punishment.

(c) This section shall not prohibit:
    (1) The use of reasonable physical force necessary to maintain order and control in a school or classroom setting;
    (2) The use of reasonable physical force necessary to quell a disturbance threatening physical injury to persons or damage to property;
    (3) The use of reasonable physical force necessary for self-defense or the defense of others;
    (4) The use of reasonable physical force necessary to obtain possession of a weapon or other dangerous object within a student's control;
    (5) The use of reasonable physical force necessary to protect a student from self-harm.

SECTION 2. ENFORCEMENT

(a) Each school district shall adopt policies prohibiting corporal punishment consistent with this Act within 90 days of the effective date.

(b) Violations of this section may result in disciplinary action against the employee, up to and including termination.

(c) Nothing in this section shall be construed to limit a parent's right to pursue civil remedies.

SECTION 3. EFFECTIVE DATE

This Act shall take effect on [DATE].
