For parents and students

After a paddling: what a family can do

What to do in the first forty-eight hours after a child is paddled at school, what your state's statute actually says, the five routes that work, the deadlines, and when a lawsuit is realistic. General information, not legal advice.

Download as a PDF The Safe Discipline Program kit

This is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before you act on anything here, talk to a lawyer licensed in your state. The section on finding one is below.

Your child came home and told you an adult at school hit them with a board. Or the school called to tell you. You are angry, you may be frightened, and the people who did it are the people your child has to face tomorrow morning.

This guide is for parents, and for students old enough to read it, in the seventeen states where school paddling is still legal. It covers what to do in the first two days, what the law in your state actually says, the five routes that have a real chance of changing something, and what each one costs in money, time and odds. It is honest about the hard part. In most of these states a paddling done the way the school board's policy says has been held lawful, even when it left bruises. The routes that work are the ones that test whether the policy was followed, protect a child with a disability, put the record in public hands, and change the policy so it does not happen to the next child.

The first forty-eight hours

Do these in order. Each one creates a record that cannot be created later.

  1. Photograph the injuries, with a date. Take the photos the same day, in good light, with something in the frame that fixes the date: that day's mail, a phone screen showing the date, a dated handwritten note. Take them again the next morning and the morning after. Bruises darken and spread over two or three days, and the second-day photos are often the ones that matter. Keep the originals on the phone and do not edit them.
  2. Take your child to a doctor the same day, and get the record. Urgent care, the pediatrician or the emergency room. Tell the clinician plainly what happened and ask that the record describe each mark, its size and its location, and what the child said caused it. Ask for a copy of the visit record before you leave, or download it from the patient portal that day. Doctors and nurses are required by law to report suspected child abuse, so a report to the state child protection agency may follow; that is not something you need to prevent. If the bruising is heavy, ask for a referral to a child abuse pediatrician. Their written opinion carries weight that a parent's photographs do not.
  3. Write down your child's account, once, in their own words. The same evening. Who struck them, where, with what, how many times, what was said before and after, who else was in the room, and whether anyone asked for their side first. Write the names and titles of the adult who did it and of any witness, as your child knows them. Do not correct or lead, and do not interview your child again and again; repeated questioning changes the account and wears the child down. Date and sign the page.
  4. Ask the school, in writing, for the records. Email the principal and copy the superintendent that day or the next morning. Ask for five things: the incident report or corporal punishment record form for your child; the board policy and the student handbook section on corporal punishment; any consent, permission or refusal form on file for your child; the witness log, or the name and title of the witness; and the name of the person who administered it. Ask for electronic copies and say that this is also a request under your state's public records law. Keep every reply. A school that cannot produce a consent form in a state that requires one, or a witness record where the statute requires a witness, has just told you something important.
  5. Do not sign anything. Not a consent form offered after the fact, not an incident acknowledgment, not a form saying your child's behavior was explained to you, not a release. Do not agree to a meeting with no record. If you meet, bring another adult, take notes, and follow up with an email that says what was said.
  6. Withdraw consent and prohibit it, in writing. In the same email, state that you do not consent to corporal punishment of your child, that you revoke any consent the school believes it has, and that you want this on file for the school year. In Texas the statute recognizes exactly this signed statement. In Missouri, Louisiana and Florida the school needed your permission first, and its absence is your strongest fact. Everywhere else, board policy almost always honors a parent's written refusal, and a school that paddles a child over a written refusal has stepped outside its own rules.

Two things not to do yet. Do not post the photographs publicly; a lawyer may want them first, and a public post will be used to say you are after attention. And do not take the school's first account as the last word. The incident report is written by the people who did it.

What the right column means. Nearly every one of these states protects the adult who paddles a child as long as the paddling stays inside the board's policy: by an explicit immunity clause, by declaring it "not abuse", by barring the district from disciplining the employee, or by treating reasonable discipline as justified. The protection runs out at the edge of the policy. A paddling with no witness where the statute requires one, without the warning the statute requires, on a student with a disability in a state that forbids it, without a consent form in a state that requires one, or with force far beyond what the policy allows, is where every route below gets stronger.

The five routes that work

Each route is described the same way: who it is for, what it costs, how long it takes, and the honest odds. None of them is a lottery ticket. The two most likely to change something are the first and the third.

1. The public records request

Who it is for. Every family, in every state, on day one.

What it costs. Nothing to send. Copy fees are usually small, and the template asks the district to call you before charging more than an amount you set.

How long it takes. Each state's records law sets a response time, from a few business days to a few weeks. The district must cite a specific exemption for anything it withholds.

What you get. The policy the paddling had to follow. The form the school was supposed to fill out. The consent or refusal form on file, or the admission that there is none. How many times the district paddled children this year and last, by school. Whether what it told the federal government in its civil rights data matches. This is the evidence for every other route, and the district has to produce it to you, not to a lawyer.

How. Use the records request template, cite your state's records law (the template tells you which), and send it to the records officer named on the district website. Arkansas, Tennessee and Alabama answer records requests only from their own residents; as a resident, you qualify. If you are helping a family from outside the state, the FOIA Request Exchange lends a resident's standing.

Honest odds. High. Districts answer records requests because the law makes them. The request does not by itself get your child anything. It is the step that turns a story into a file.

2. A federal civil rights complaint

Who it is for. A child with a disability, with an Individualized Education Program or a Section 504 plan or who should have been evaluated for one. Or a family who can point to a pattern: the paddle falls on Black children, or on boys, in a way the numbers show. A paddling by itself is not a federal civil rights violation. The complaint has to be about discrimination, or about retaliation for complaining.

What it costs. Nothing. No lawyer is needed. Anyone may file, including on behalf of a child.

The deadline. One hundred eighty days from the last act of discrimination. The Office for Civil Rights can grant a waiver for good cause. Do not count on one.

How. File online through the Office for Civil Rights complaint portal, or with the fillable complaint form, or by a signed letter to OCR@ed.gov that names the district, describes what happened and when, and says why you believe it was discrimination. The agency's own instructions list what a letter must contain. For a child under eighteen a parent or guardian signs the consent form; if the office asks for it and does not receive it within twenty calendar days, the complaint is closed. You do not have to use the district's grievance process first. If you do, you then have sixty days after it ends to file.

What happens next. The office acknowledges the complaint and decides whether to open it. If it does, it investigates as a neutral fact finder, or offers a facilitated resolution. The usual outcome of a successful complaint is a written resolution agreement in which the district changes what it does and the office monitors it. The office does not award money to your child and does not represent you. It does not promise a timeline; plan on months.

A second route for a child with an IEP. The state education agency must take a signed written complaint that the district violated the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, if the violation was within the past year, and must resolve it within sixty days. In Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma and Tennessee the statute itself forbids or restricts paddling a student with a disability, so the complaint can cite state law too.

Honest odds. For a child with a disability in a state that forbids it, good: the facts are usually not in dispute and the fix is a policy the district can sign. For a pattern complaint, it depends on the numbers, which the records request and the federal civil rights data for your district on the map give you.

3. A complaint to the district and the state education agency

Who it is for. Every family. This is also the route that most often ends in a changed policy.

What it costs. Nothing.

How. Write to the superintendent and the board, not only the principal. State what happened, attach the photographs and the medical record, name the policy provisions you believe were not followed, and ask for three things in writing by a date you set: an investigation, a copy of the findings, and an assurance that your child will not be paddled again. Ask to speak at the next board meeting. Public comment is yours by right, the meeting is recorded, and three minutes with photographs in hand is worth more than a dozen phone calls. Then write to the state education agency. It supervises districts' compliance with state law, and in a state with a consent, witness, documentation or disability rule in the statute, a violation is squarely its business. The table in the lawyer section gives each agency's complaint page.

How long it takes. Weeks for a district response, if your letter sets the date. A board policy change takes two readings, usually two months.

Honest odds. The district will probably defend its employee. But a documented complaint, a public comment and a records file have moved boards. The project's district record counts more than eleven hundred school boards in these seventeen states whose written policy prohibits corporal punishment, and every one of those policies was a vote by a board that once allowed it.

4. A criminal complaint

Who it is for. A child with injuries beyond temporary redness, a paddling with force or in a manner outside the policy, an adult who struck in anger, a paddling with no witness, or a child the statute protects.

What it costs. Nothing. Report to the local police or sheriff, or go directly to the district attorney's office. The doctor's report may already have brought in the state child protection agency.

How long it takes. An investigation may take weeks; a charging decision, months.

Honest odds. Low for a paddling that followed the policy. Most of these states' laws treat reasonable school discipline as justified, and some grant criminal immunity outright, so a prosecutor reads the statute and the policy before the photographs. In Overton, Texas in 2023, the charge against a principal was dismissed although a child abuse physician called the bruising consistent with abuse, because the policy was followed and the mother was present and consenting. Charges do happen when the facts are worse: in Jackson County, Tennessee, a teacher and an assistant principal were indicted in November 2023 after two paddlings injured a student. A report also creates a dated official record and puts a second set of eyes on the school, which has value even when no charge follows.

5. A civil lawsuit

Who it is for. A family with a serious injury, clear evidence that the policy or the statute was not followed, or a child with a disability in a state that protects them, and a lawyer who has read the records and said yes.

State court first. The claims are assault, battery and negligence against the employee, and negligence against the district. Three walls stand in the way. First, the immunity clauses in the table above. Second, governmental immunity: in most of these states a school district can be sued only as far as its tort claims act allows, often only up to its insurance, with caps on damages. Third, the notice-of-claim rules in the next section, which can close the courthouse within months. A lawyer who has sued a school district before knows where the doors are.

Federal court second, and only in some states. Seven federal appeals circuits allow a suit under the federal civil rights statute, Section 1983, when a paddling "shocks the conscience". Those courts describe the bar as malice or sadism and injury far beyond anything discipline could justify, not an unwise excess of zeal. Among the paddling states, that rule covers Alabama, Georgia and Florida (Eleventh Circuit), Arkansas and Missouri (Eighth), Kentucky and Tennessee (Sixth), Oklahoma, Kansas and Wyoming (Tenth), and North Carolina and South Carolina (Fourth). Indiana (Seventh Circuit) and Arizona (Ninth) analyze excessive school force as an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment instead; the test is different but the bar for an ordinary paddling is still high. The Fifth Circuit, which covers Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, holds that there is no federal claim at all for school corporal punishment where the state offers a tort or criminal remedy, and the Supreme Court has declined to review that rule repeatedly, most recently in January 2024. In those three states a federal suit over a paddling will be dismissed. Do not pay anyone to file one.

What it costs. Lawyers take strong injury cases on contingency, typically a third or more of any recovery plus costs. Weak cases are not taken, which is itself information worth having. In North Carolina a suit the court finds frivolous can cost you the district's attorney fees; see below.

How long it takes. One to three years to a settlement or a trial.

Honest odds. For a policy-compliant paddling, poor, in every state. For a paddling outside the policy with documented injury, or on a protected child, real. This project found no published settlement or verdict for a school paddling in the United States between 2022 and 2026. That does not mean none exist. It means there is no headline number to expect.

The deadlines

Two clocks run. The limitation period is the time to file suit. The notice-of-claim rule, where a state has one, is shorter: a written notice to the district within months, without which the suit is barred no matter how strong. A child's age pauses some clocks and not others, and in several states the notice deadline is not paused at all. The citations link to each state's own statute site. Read them with a lawyer, early; the shortest clock is the one that matters.

The fee-shifting risk in North Carolina

North Carolina is the one state with a statute aimed at families who sue over a paddling. G.S. 6-21.4 says, in full:

In any civil action brought against a public school principal or teacher as defined in G.S. 115C-390 arising or resulting from the use of corporal punishment, upon a determination that the principal or teacher has prevailed and that the plaintiff's action was frivolous or without substantial merit, the presiding judge may, in his discretion, allow a reasonable attorney fee to the duly licensed attorney representing the principal or teacher. The attorney's fee shall be taxed as part of the court costs.

Read it carefully before fearing it. The fee is discretionary, and it requires two findings: the educator won, and the suit was frivolous or without substantial merit. A suit that loses on the facts is not frivolous. But in North Carolina every district has already prohibited corporal punishment by policy, so a paddling there is outside the rules from the start, and a lawyer will want to see the records before filing anything, which is the order this guide recommends everywhere.

How to find a lawyer

Start with the three free doors. The state bar's lawyer referral service connects you to a lawyer for a short first consultation at a set fee or none. The state's protection and advocacy organization represents children with disabilities at no charge and knows the school districts well. Legal aid takes families who qualify by income. Law school clinics in education law or children's rights take a few cases a year. When you call, ask one question first: have you sued a school district before? Then ask about the notice-of-claim deadline before anything else, and bring the file from the first forty-eight hours.

What a lawful paddling looks like in court

Families should not expect what the law will not give, so here is the case that shows the shape of it.

In 2023 a principal in Overton, Texas paddled a student hard enough to leave bruising. The mother was present and had consented. A child abuse physician described the bruising as consistent with abuse. The principal was charged with assault. In October 2023 a Rusk County judge dismissed the charge, because the district's policy had been followed and the mother had consented. Two months of investigation and news coverage ended with no finding against anyone.

Read it the way a court would. The bruises were not the question. The questions were whether the policy allowed it, whether the policy was followed, and whether the parent had agreed. In Texas, as in the other states in the table, those three answers decide the case, and the statute says the employee may not even be disciplined by the district for force the law calls justified. That is why this guide spends so many words on the records, the consent form and the witness: they are the facts that decide whether your case is this case or a different one.

Turn the incident into a policy change

The one outcome that is in reach for every family, in every state, is that the board stops. Boards are elected, their meetings are public, and the policy that allowed the paddling can be replaced at the next two meetings. Ask for it.

  • Bring the board brief for your state and the model policy from the Safe Discipline Program kit. The model policy is written so a district's attorney can adopt it with the district's own numbering, and the kit includes the letters to staff and families and the training that replaces the paddle.
  • Find your district's current written policy on its state page, quoted and linked, so no one at the meeting can say the policy is not what it is.
  • Ask the board one question in public: has the district's insurer been told that corporal punishment is in use, and is it a rated exposure? Then ask for the answer in writing.
  • Show the board the film, four minutes, made for exactly this meeting.
  • When the board votes to stop, the district is recognized as a Safe School, with a certificate and a press release it can use. Write to anthony@175g.com and it will be arranged.
