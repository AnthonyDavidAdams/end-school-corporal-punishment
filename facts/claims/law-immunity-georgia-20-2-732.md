Section 20-2-732 as displayed on the official portal: "No principal or teacher who shall administer corporal punishment to a pupil or pupils under his care and supervision in conformity with the policies and regulations of the area, county, or independent board of education employing him and in accordance also with this subpart shall be held accountable or liable in any criminal or civil action based upon the administering of corporal punishment where the corporal punishment is administered in good faith and is not excessive or unduly severe." The portal's annotation notes Peterson v. Baker, 504 F.3d 1331 (11th Cir. 2007), applying the immunity absent "actual malice or an intent to injure."

Section 20-2-731 opening and requirements (1) and (2) as displayed: "An area, county, or independent board of education may, upon the adoption of written policies, authorize any principal or teacher employed by the board to administer, in the exercise of his sound discretion, corporal punishment on any pupil or pupils placed under his supervision in order to maintain proper control and discipline. Any such authorization shall be subject to the following requirements: (1) The corporal punishment shall not be excessive or unduly severe; (2) Corporal punishment shall never be used as a first line of punishment for misbehavior unless the pupil was informed beforehand that specific misbehavior could occasion ..." The portal's search view truncated the section there; the remaining requirements (a witness informed beforehand, a written explanation to the parent on request, and the exemption for a child whose parent files a physician's statement) are recorded in data/states/GA.yaml from an earlier read and were not re-read on this date because the portal's full-document view sits behind a CAPTCHA.

Standard words: "good faith," "not excessive or unduly severe," "in conformity with the policies," "sound discretion," "proper control and discipline." Georgia is the clearest statutory statement that immunity stops at excess.
