# Liability: what the law actually does when a paddling goes wrong

Advocates lead with liability. Before doing so, know where it is real and where it is not; the sourced claims are `facts/claims/law-ingraham-v-wright-1977.md`, `law-circuit-split-fifth-circuit.md` and `litigation-overton-isd-2023-dismissal.md`.

## Federal claims

*Ingraham v. Wright* (1977) closed the Eighth Amendment and pre-paddling due process routes and left substantive due process open. Since then:

- **Seven circuits** (2d, 3d, 4th, 6th, 8th, 10th, 11th) allow a section 1983 claim when corporal punishment "shocks the conscience": *Hall v. Tawney* (4th Cir. 1980), *Garcia v. Miera* (10th Cir. 1987), *Neal v. Fulton County* (11th Cir. 2000, a coach blinded a student with a weight lock). The bar is malice or sadism, not an unwise excess of zeal; ordinary policy-compliant paddling does not meet it.
- **Two circuits** (7th, 9th) analyze it as a Fourth Amendment seizure.
- **The Fifth Circuit** (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, which together are about 45 percent of all US paddling) holds that no federal claim exists at all where state tort or criminal remedies are available: *Fee v. Herndon* (1990), *T.O. v. Fort Bend ISD* (2021, a first-grader with disabilities in a chokehold), *S.B. v. Jefferson Parish* (2023, a nonverbal autistic child slapped). The Supreme Court denied review in *T.O.* (June 2022) and *S.B.* (January 2024, Institute for Justice petition); two Fifth Circuit judges have urged their own court or the Supreme Court to revisit the rule.

Consequence: in the three states with the most paddling, the federal liability threat is the weakest in the country. Do not tell a Mississippi board it will be sued in federal court.

## State law

Every remaining state grants some immunity: Alabama § 16-28A-1 (immune if policy followed), Arkansas § 6-17-112 (none for the four protected disability categories), Georgia § 20-2-732 (good faith, not excessive), Mississippi § 37-11-57(2) (absent bad faith), Missouri 160.261.10 (spanking by certificated staff with a witness "is not abuse"), Texas § 22.0512 (district may not discipline an employee for justified force), Wyoming § 21-4-308(b) (civil and criminal immunity), Louisiana R.S. 17:416.1(C) (board must defend and indemnify). North Carolina G.S. 6-21.4 allows attorney fees against frivolous corporal punishment suits.

Criminal cases follow the same pattern. The Overton ISD principal's 2023 assault charge was dismissed because policy was followed and the mother consented, though the bruising was called consistent with abuse. The Jackson County, Tennessee indictments of November 2023 are the exception and their outcome is unknown.

## What liability arguments survive

1. **The immunity runs out at the policy's edge.** Every statute conditions immunity on following board policy. A district whose policy is vague, whose witness rules are ignored, or whose consent forms are not on file has staff with no immunity. The records request (`templates/public-records-request.md`) tests this.
2. **Students with disabilities.** Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma and Tennessee strip immunity or prohibit the practice outright for these students; Mississippi districts reported 211 such paddlings after their ban. That is a real exposure and an OCR complaint.
3. **Insurance and settlements.** No published paddling settlement was found for 2022-26, which cuts both ways: there is no headline number to cite, and there is no evidence the risk is priced. Ask the district's insurer, in a public meeting, whether corporal punishment is a rated exposure.
4. **The real cost is the administrator's time and the story.** Overton ISD spent two months under investigation and in the news for a paddling that was lawful. That, not a verdict, is the argument that works with a superintendent.
