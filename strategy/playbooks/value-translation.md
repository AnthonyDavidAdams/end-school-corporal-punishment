# Translating the value: the Safe School ask in the superintendent's own terms

Built 2026-10-27 from Anthony's "Translating The Value" prompt (a coaching-sales frame: take each component of the
package, translate it into what the buyer values, across Time, Effort, Energy, Convenience, Hassle, Stress, Money
and Results, toward and away from, then invent new components that raise the perceived value). Applied here to the
one thing we sell: a board vote to put an explicit prohibition in writing, bought with the free Safe Discipline
Program kit. Numbers in this file either come from `facts/claims/` (id in brackets) or are deliberately absent;
`strategy/liability.md` governs what may be said about lawsuits. No em dashes in copy.

## The avatar

The superintendent of a district of 500 to 5,000 students in a permitting state, usually rural, often the only
central-office administrator with a policy brief. Reported zero or near-zero paddlings in the last federal count
but the manual still says it may be done (the 827 "policy lag" districts, 1.5 million students). Five to seven
board members who are neighbors, a solicitor on retainer by the hour, a handbook that was last really read in
2011. Values, in order: no surprises at a board meeting; no story in the regional paper; staff who stay; parents
who trust the office; the state leaving them alone; being seen as a district that does things right.

Toward: recognition, a clean story they control, a finished task, being ahead of the state.
Away from: the paddling that goes wrong under a new hire who read the manual; two months under investigation for
something that was technically lawful (Overton ISD, 2023 [litigation-overton-isd-2023-dismissal]); an OCR complaint
over a student with a disability; a reporter asking why the policy still says "may"; being the last board in the
county, or the state, to act.

A second avatar, the board member, is the same person with less time and more exposure: their name is on the
vote, and on the record page the public reads.

## Translations by domain

**Time.** The policy committee work, the drafting to statute and the communications are done. What is left is one
reading and one vote. The opt-out forms, the witness rules, the parent notices and the annual report to the state
(North Carolina requires a report by race, sex and disability under G.S. 115C-390.4) stop being anyone's job.
Toward: "Done in one meeting." Away: "Every year you keep the policy, someone keeps the forms."

**Effort.** Adopt as written, or hand the solicitor a tracked-changes edit of your own policy code. No committee,
no survey, no working group. Toward: "The board votes on words that already match what you do." Away: "A policy
nobody follows still has to be defended by someone."

**Energy.** The kit's first rule is to say it was a mistake once, plainly, and be done; districts that say "times
have changed" spend a year relitigating. Toward: "Say it once and it's over." Away: "The 'may' in the manual is a
question you will be asked again every time a paddling story runs anywhere in the state."

**Convenience.** One PDF, in the order it is used, with the model language in the same policy code your manual
already uses (NCSBA 4302 in North Carolina, FO(LOCAL) in Texas, the state model elsewhere). Toward: "It drops
into your manual where the old one sits." Away: "Writing it yourself means finding the statute, the model, the
disability exemptions, and the consent rule, again."

**Hassle.** Ends the records requests (ours and others') asking where the policy lives, the parent opt-out
paperwork, the witness and notice bookkeeping, and the annual state report. Toward: "Nothing to file, nothing to
report." Away: "The paperwork exists to protect staff who paddle. If nobody paddles, it protects no one and costs
a clerk's week."

**Stress.** The immunity statutes all run out at the policy's edge: a permitting policy with no training and no
forms on file is the worst position, because the next new hire can read the manual and act on it, lawfully, and
the district owns what follows. Overton ISD spent two months under investigation and in the news for a paddling
its policy allowed. Toward: "No new hire can surprise you." Away: "The one thing that still can go wrong is the
one thing the manual still permits." (Do not say "you will be sued"; `strategy/liability.md` explains why, and in
Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi it is not even true.)

**Money.** Be honest here: there is no published paddling settlement in the last five years to wave around
[liability.md §3]. What is real: a solicitor's hours to draft; a vendor's price for fourteen hours of behavior
training; an administrator's two months answering an investigation; a clerk's time on forms and reports. All of
that is in the package or ended by it, and the package is free. The one money line that lands with a board: "Ask
your carrier, in open session, whether corporal punishment is a rated exposure. If they say no, you are carrying a
risk nobody priced. If they say yes, you are paying for a practice you don't use." Toward: "Free, and it ends
paid work." Away: "You are paying, in time, for a policy you don't use."

**Results.** No study has found a benefit; on average the practice increases the behavior it is meant to stop
[study-gershoff-grogan-kaylor-2016, study-visser-2022-school-meta-analysis]. Students with disabilities are
paddled at about twice their share [crdc-disability-share]. For a district already at zero, the results are
reputational: the certificate, the press release, the listing, the peers. In North Carolina, 94 of 111 boards on
the record prohibit it in writing and no district has used it since 2018; in Texas, 300 boards have adopted the
sentence. Toward: "Join the list." Away: "Be the last one on the other list."

## Connecting it back to the investment

The price is a vote: one agenda item, fifteen minutes, and the superintendent's say-so. For that the district gets
the policy, the motion, the staff script, the family letter, the student script, the history page, fourteen hours
of training, a year of help, a certificate, a press release, a listing, a coach for every teacher and parent, and a
parent handout. Said in the avatar's voice: "The whole package is the work a policy change normally costs you.
It is done, it is free, and the only thing left is to put it on the agenda."

## New elements that raise the value (and most cost us nothing)

1. **The redline of their own policy.** We edit their actual policy code (4355, FO(LOCAL), 5.14) in tracked
   changes, so the solicitor reviews instead of drafts. Highest-value single add; we already have the quote and code.
2. **The one-page board slide with their own numbers.** Their CRDC line, the peer count ("94 of 111"), the
   model sentence, and the vote language. Rendered from the record per district.
3. **Sixty seconds of superintendent's remarks** for the vote, written to the kit's first rule.
4. **The press release, placed.** Not just written: we pitch it to the local outlet from `data/media/press-list.jsonl`
   so the first story is the positive one. "Your local paper runs the good version first."
5. **A printed certificate, mailed**, plus the digital one. A thing for the board room wall.
6. **A thirty-minute call with a superintendent who did it.** The Kansas superintendent's "harvest litigation" line
   came from one; peer calls close boards that briefs do not.
7. **The parent FAQ for the district's own website**, written and ready to paste.
8. **The questions sheet**: the questions staff, parents and board members will ask, with answers, one page each.
9. **The insurance letter**, pre-written, asking the carrier whether the practice is a rated exposure.
10. **Annual Safe School renewal** with a dated badge, so the recognition is a standing thing, not a one-off.
11. **A handbook consistency check** of their student handbook against the new policy, delivered as a short list.
12. **A line for the strategic plan or accreditation report**, written to the language those documents use.
13. **The first-year "ask the coach" card** for staff, with the coach link and the office response ladder on one side.
