# Memorandum: is corporal punishment necessary?

**To:** The Board of Education, [DISTRICT NAME] School District, Missouri
**From:** EarthPilot Safe Schools Project
**Date:** October 29, 2026
**Re:** The board's own standard for corporal punishment, and what the evidence says about it

*This memo is a model for discussion. It is not legal advice and it is not a filing. Every figure in it is kept in a sourced fact file at earthpilot.org/kids/resources/.*

## The board's own standard

Missouri law does not authorize corporal punishment; it leaves the decision to each board and then limits what the board may protect. Section 160.261 of the Revised Statutes provides that spanking, "when administered by certificated personnel and in the presence of a witness who is an employee of the school district," or "the use of reasonable force to protect persons or property, when administered by personnel of a school district in a reasonable manner in accordance with the local board of education's written policy of discipline, is not abuse." The same section forbids any corporal punishment "without a parent or guardian being notified and providing written permission," and shields staff from suit only "when acting in conformity with the established policies developed by each board." The policy wording that dozens of Missouri boards use says: "Corporal punishment should be used only after other methods have failed and when there is reason to believe it will be helpful in maintaining discipline or in the development of the student's character and power of self-control." Behind both sits the rule the Supreme Court described in nineteen seventy-seven: a teacher may use the force a teacher "reasonably believes to be necessary" for the child's control, training or education, judged by all the circumstances, including "the availability of less severe but equally effective means of discipline."

## What each word asks

*Reasonable* asks whether a careful person, knowing what is now known, would choose this method. *Necessary* asks whether it does something no milder method would do. *Only after other methods have failed* and *reason to believe it will be helpful*, the board's own words, ask the board to compare methods and to expect a result. These are questions about results, and results can be measured.

## What the research answers

- **Does it make children behave?** A review of seventy-five studies covering one hundred sixty thousand children (Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor, two thousand sixteen) found physical punishment linked to thirteen of the seventeen outcomes measured, every one for the worse and none for the better. Immediate obedience, the one thing it is supposed to produce, was not significantly improved.
- **In schools?** A review of twenty-one studies of school corporal punishment covering sixty-seven thousand children (Visser, van der Put and Assink, two thousand twenty-two) found more aggression and disruption, more anxiety and depression, and lower school performance among the students who received it.
- **Later on?** A matched comparison of more than eight hundred young adults from the states where it was legal, Missouri among them (Gershoff, Sattler and Holden, two thousand nineteen), found that those paddled in school had lower high school grades and more symptoms of depression.
- **Does it keep children engaged?** A three-year study of six hundred fifty-eight children (Font and Cage, two thousand eighteen) found that every form of physical punishment, including mild, predicted a drop in school engagement.
- **Do milder methods work?** A review of fifty-four controlled studies of classroom management programs (Korpershoek and others, two thousand sixteen) found that they improved behavior, learning and social skills. A five-year randomized trial in thirty-seven schools (Bradshaw and others, two thousand twelve) found one third fewer office discipline referrals under schoolwide positive behavior supports.
- **Does stopping make things worse?** No study has found that ending corporal punishment increases misbehavior, and no study has found that using it improves behavior (literature reviewed through September two thousand twenty-six).

Most of these studies show association, not cause, and this memo does not claim more. The board's standard does not require more. It requires reason to believe the method will be helpful, and a method with no measured benefit is not necessary when methods with measured benefit are at hand.

## Boards that manage without it

More than eleven hundred school boards in the seventeen states whose law still permits corporal punishment have a written policy prohibiting it. In Missouri, one hundred twenty-five of three hundred twenty-five boards prohibit it in writing, and seventy-one more have no policy permitting it; one hundred twenty-eight boards still permit it, eighty-two of them only with a parent's signed consent, as the statute has required since August two thousand twenty-two. Missouri districts reported one thousand two students receiving corporal punishment in the two thousand twenty-three to twenty-four school year, down from one thousand one hundred thirty-three two years earlier. The boards that stopped have the same students, the same statute and the same discipline problems as this one.

## What the law's own authors say

The American Law Institute, whose Restatement of Torts supplied the standard the Supreme Court quoted in nineteen seventy-seven, approved a new Restatement of Children and the Law in May two thousand twenty-four. Its section on school discipline says: "The use of force to punish through the infliction of pain is not necessary to maintain order or safety." The American Academy of Pediatrics asked every state to end the practice in two thousand twenty-three. The United States Secretary of Education wrote to every governor asking the same in March of that year. Seventy-nine national organizations, including both teachers' unions, the NAACP and the National PTA, signed an open letter to the same effect in two thousand sixteen.

## What this means for the board

The board's policy protects spanking only after other methods have failed and only where there is reason to believe it will help; the statute protects staff only when they act in conformity with that policy and in a reasonable manner. Before this memo, the board could say it did not know what the research found. After it, a decision to keep the policy is a decision to keep a harsher method where a milder one is known to work as well. That is the one thing the board's own words do not allow. It matters to whoever insures the district as well: a protection conditioned on reasonableness is only as good as the reasonableness.

## The ask

Adopt the sentence: "Corporal punishment is prohibited in all schools and programs operated by the [DISTRICT NAME] School District." Twenty-eight Missouri boards use a shorter form of it in policy JGA-2, "Corporal punishment is strictly prohibited as a method of discipline," and either will do. Keep every other tool the district uses. Nothing in that sentence limits the reasonable force a staff member needs to protect a student or others from harm, to take a weapon, or to keep order; the model policy that accompanies this memo says so in its next sentence.
