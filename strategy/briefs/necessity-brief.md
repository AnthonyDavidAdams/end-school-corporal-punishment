# The Necessity Argument: Why Corporal Punishment Cannot Be Reasonably Believed Necessary Once the Evidence Is Known

**A model brief for discussion. This document is not legal advice and is not a filing.** It is written for a lawyer who is weighing whether to raise the argument in a state tort case, a federal civil rights case, a state constitutional case, or a letter to a school board, and it is meant to be checked, cut and rewritten for the forum. Every authority is listed in the table at the end with the date on which this project read it at its primary source. Fact files behind each research claim live in `facts/claims/` of the End School Corporal Punishment repository, and each file names the limits of the study it records.

Prepared by the EarthPilot Safe Schools Project, October 29, 2026.

## 1. Question presented

Every statute that permits corporal punishment in a public school, and the common-law privilege behind every such statute, protects only force that is reasonable, not excessive, and reasonably believed to be necessary to maintain discipline. The published research, now spanning more than seventy-five studies of physical punishment in general and twenty-one studies of school corporal punishment in particular, has found no measured benefit from the practice and a consistent association with worse behavior, worse mental health and lower achievement, while controlled studies of ordinary classroom management programs show that milder methods produce the behavior corporal punishment is supposed to produce.

The question presented is whether a school employee who has been placed on notice of that evidence can be said to "reasonably believe" that striking a child is "necessary" for the child's control, training or education, or whether, as a matter of law, the privilege no longer reaches the practice at all.

A second question follows from the first. The privilege has always had a comparative element: a harsher method may not be used where a milder one would probably work as well. If more than eleven hundred school boards in the seventeen permitting states run their schools without corporal punishment, and no study has found that stopping it makes behavior worse, is the milder method's availability now a fact a court can take notice of?

## 2. The privilege and its limits

### 2.1 Ingraham v. Wright and the common-law rule

*Ingraham v. Wright*, 430 U.S. 651 (1977), is usually cited for what it closed: the Eighth Amendment does not apply to school paddling, and due process does not require a hearing before it, because state tort and criminal remedies suffice. It should be cited as often for what it kept. The Court described the rule it was leaving in place, at 661 to 662:

> At common law a single principle has governed the use of corporal punishment since before the American Revolution: Teachers may impose reasonable but not excessive force to discipline a child. ... The prevalent rule in this country today privileges such force as a teacher or administrator "reasonably believes to be necessary for (the child's) proper control, training, or education." Restatement (Second) of Torts § 147(2) (1965); see id., § 153(2). To the extent that the force is excessive or unreasonable, the educator in virtually all States is subject to possible civil and criminal liability.

And then, in the passage that this brief is built on:

> All of the circumstances are to be taken into account in determining whether the punishment is reasonable in a particular case. Among the most important considerations are the seriousness of the offense, the attitude and past behavior of the child, the nature and severity of the punishment, the age and strength of the child, and the availability of less severe but equally effective means of discipline. Id., at 290-291; Restatement (Second) of Torts § 150, Comments c-e, p. 268 (1965).

The "Id." is Harper and James, *Law of Torts* § 3.20 (1956). The sentence is therefore the Supreme Court's own statement of the common-law factors, drawn from the leading treatise and the Restatement together. The Court's holding that state remedies are adequate rests on the premise that those remedies apply this standard. A court that is asked to apply the standard is doing exactly what *Ingraham* said courts would do.

Two further points from *Ingraham* matter. The Court recognized a "constitutionally protected liberty interest" in freedom from bodily punishment (at 674), and it expressly left open whether excessive corporal punishment violates substantive due process (at 659 n.12). Fifty years of circuit litigation have run in that opening; see section 3.

### 2.2 Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 147 and 150

The Restatement text is sold by the American Law Institute and is not freely posted; this project has read it only as quoted in opinions, and the fact file `law-restatement-second-torts-147-150` records the words as the courts give them.

Section 147(2), as quoted in *Ingraham*, privileges force the teacher "reasonably believes to be necessary for (the child's) proper control, training, or education." The privilege therefore has two elements the plaintiff can test: a belief, and the reasonableness of that belief measured against what the actor knew or should have known.

Section 150 supplies the reasonableness factors. *In re Rodney C.*, 91 Misc. 2d 677, 680 to 681 (N.Y. Fam. Ct. 1977), a parental case decided the same year as *Ingraham*, quotes them in the Restatement's own words. Subdivision (b) lists "the age, sex, physical and mental condition of the child." Comment d: a parent is not privileged "to use a means to compel obedience if a less severe method appears likely to be equally effective." Comment e: "a punishment may not be unnecessarily degrading." Section 151 adds that force "applied primarily for any purpose other than proper training or education of the child or for the preservation of discipline is not privileged."

Comment d is the comparative rule. It does not ask whether the harsher method worked; it asks whether a milder one would probably have worked as well, and if so it withdraws the privilege from the harsher one. Read with § 147(2), the rule is this: the actor must reasonably believe the force is necessary, and force is not necessary where a less severe method appears likely to be equally effective.

Two cautions for the drafter. First, the phrase "less severe but equally effective means of discipline" is the Supreme Court's wording, not the Restatement's; when quoting the Restatement itself, use *Rodney C.*'s "less severe method appears likely to be equally effective." Deana Pollard Sacks's 2009 article reproduces the Court's phrase at 1174 n.34, and it has been repeated from her since. Second, *Rodney C.* applies § 150 to a parent; *Ingraham* applies the same sections to teachers through §§ 147(2) and 153(2), and the ALI's 2019 draft, discussed in section 7, says the teacher's privilege is the narrower of the two.

Three features of this table carry the argument.

First, none of the ten statutes protects force that is unreasonable or excessive, and none defines reasonableness. The content of the word comes from the common law, which is to say from *Ingraham* and § 150.

Second, three statutes write necessity into the text. Texas Penal Code § 9.62 is the Restatement rule verbatim: force is justified only "when and to the degree the actor reasonably believes the force is necessary." Mississippi defines corporal punishment as force "as may be necessary to maintain discipline," so that force not necessary to maintain discipline is, by definition, outside the immunity. Tennessee's 2023 amendment requires written proof that milder steps were taken first, which is comment d made into a checklist.

Third, Alabama and Arkansas condition immunity on compliance with board policy rather than on reasonableness. That moves the inquiry to the policy, and nearly every permitting policy in those states itself says "reasonable." A model policy used by dozens of Missouri districts (policy 2670) says corporal punishment "should be used only after other methods have failed and when there is reason to believe it will be helpful in maintaining discipline." That sentence is comment d in the board's own words, and it is the sentence this argument asks the board to apply.

## 3. Why no United States court has reached the question

### 3.1 Sacks's diagnosis

Deana Pollard Sacks, *State Actors Beating Children: A Call for Judicial Relief*, 42 U.C. Davis L. Rev. 1165 (2009), set out the problem at 1169:

> The intent-based executive deprivation model employed in school corporal punishment cases for the past thirty years was rendered unconstitutional nearly two decades ago, but remains the majority rule. The more pressing issue relevant to all instances of school corporal punishment, its social efficacy and propriety, remains unexamined by the judiciary, despite a wealth of social science research ...

Her point is procedural as much as substantive. After *Ingraham*, the federal courts built a case-by-case excessive-force model under substantive due process in which the question is whether a particular paddling was so severe, so disproportionate, and so malicious that it "shocks the conscience." That model takes the practice as given and asks only about the outlier. It never asks whether the practice does what it is for, because the plaintiff's own theory concedes that a routine paddling is lawful and complains only of this one. Sacks wrote before the circuit split hardened; her diagnosis has held.

### 3.2 The circuit split

The fact file `law-circuit-split-fifth-circuit` maps the federal landscape as of January 2024. Seven circuits allow a § 1983 claim for corporal punishment that shocks the conscience: *Hall v. Tawney*, 621 F.2d 607 (4th Cir. 1980); *Garcia v. Miera*, 817 F.2d 650 (10th Cir. 1987); *Webb v. McCullough*, 828 F.2d 1151 (6th Cir. 1987); *Metzger v. Osbeck*, 841 F.2d 518 (3d Cir. 1988); *Wise v. Pea Ridge School District*, 855 F.2d 560 (8th Cir. 1988); *Neal v. Fulton County Board of Education*, 229 F.3d 1069 (11th Cir. 2000); *Johnson v. Newburgh Enlarged School District*, 239 F.3d 246 (2d Cir. 2001). Two circuits analyze it as a Fourth Amendment seizure: *Wallace v. Batavia School District*, 68 F.3d 1010 (7th Cir. 1995); *Doe v. Hawaii Department of Education*, 334 F.3d 906 (9th Cir. 2003). The Fifth Circuit alone holds that no federal claim exists where state remedies are available: *Fee v. Herndon*, 900 F.2d 804 (5th Cir. 1990); *T.O. v. Fort Bend Independent School District*, 2 F.4th 407 (5th Cir. 2021), cert. denied June 13, 2022; *S.B. v. Jefferson Parish School Board* (5th Cir. 2023), cert. denied January 8, 2024.

Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, the three Fifth Circuit states, together accounted for about two fifths of all reported school corporal punishment in the United States in 2023-24 (8,077 of 19,810 students in this project's state extract of the federal data). In the states where the most children are struck, the federal courthouse door is shut, and the only forum is state court, applying state law, which is to say applying the standard in section 2.

The reporter citations above were each resolved through CourtListener's citation lookup on October 29, 2026; the characterization of each circuit's rule is taken from the fact file's listing and from the Institute for Justice's certiorari petition in *S.B.*, No. 23-440, which maps the split. The drafter should read each opinion before filing.

### 3.3 Why the split makes the state-law question live

The shocks-the-conscience model has three consequences that the necessity argument turns to its advantage.

First, it has left the state tort privilege untouched. The federal courts have been careful to say that state remedies remain; *Ingraham* rests on it and *Fee* insists on it. A state court applying its own tort law is not bound by any federal holding on what "reasonable" means in § 147(2).

Second, in the seven substantive due process circuits the question of whether force was "necessary" is already part of the test. The Fourth Circuit's formulation in *Hall v. Tawney*, 621 F.2d 607 (4th Cir. 1980), asks "whether the force applied caused injury so severe, was so disproportionate to the need presented, and was so inspired by malice or sadism rather than a merely careless or unwise excess of zeal that it amounted to a brutal and inhumane abuse of official power literally shocking to the conscience." A need is a necessity. The same paragraph of *Hall* says that a state tort claim, by contrast, "may well turn on whether 'ten licks rather than five' were excessive," which is the court's own acknowledgment that the state-law privilege is where the fine questions of reasonableness are decided. If the evidence shows there is no need that corporal punishment meets and a milder method does not, the proportionality inquiry has a new input.

Third, the Fifth Circuit's rule is that state remedies are adequate. Two of its judges have urged reconsideration, and the Institute for Justice petition laid the split before the Supreme Court. An argument in a Texas, Louisiana or Mississippi state court that the state tort privilege does not reach routine corporal punishment is the argument that tests whether those remedies are in fact adequate. Either the state court applies its own standard honestly, or the premise of *Fee* is shown to be false. Both outcomes are useful.

## 4. The evidence on efficacy and harm

This section states each finding with its effect size and with the limit that the fact file records. The limits are not footnotes; they are part of the argument, because the standard is reasonable belief, and a reasonable actor knows what the evidence does and does not show.

### 4.1 No measured benefit

**Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor (2016)**, *Journal of Family Psychology* 30(4), a meta-analysis of seventy-five studies covering 160,927 children, found spanking significantly associated with thirteen of seventeen outcomes examined, every one in the direction of harm and none in the direction of benefit, including aggression (d = .37), mental health problems (d = .53) and later victimization by physical abuse (d = .64). The outcome most relevant to necessity is immediate compliance: the association (d = .14) was not statistically significant. Physical punishment did not reliably produce even the short-term obedience that is its stated purpose. Limit: the studies are correlational and concern parental spanking, not school paddling. The file is `study-gershoff-grogan-kaylor-2016`.

**No post-ban outcome study.** As of September 2026, no study has found that ending corporal punishment increases misbehavior, and no study has found that using it improves behavior; there is no United States natural-experiment study of suspensions or achievement before and after a state or district ban. The absence cuts both ways and the fact file says so: the stronger causal claim that bans improve behavior is not supported either. What the absence does establish is that the proponent of necessity has no evidence of benefit to point to. The file is `evidence-no-post-ban-outcome-study`, which rests on Gershoff's 2017 global review and the 2023 American Academy of Pediatrics statement.

### 4.2 Consistent harm, in schools specifically

**Visser, van der Put and Assink (2022)**, *Children* 9(3):383, is the meta-analysis specific to school corporal punishment. Across twenty-one studies and about 67,400 children it found school corporal punishment associated with more externalizing behavior (r = .27), more internalizing problems (r = .16), and, across twenty studies and 977,367 children, lower school performance (r = −.11). Externalizing behavior is aggression, defiance and disruption: the conduct corporal punishment is used to stop is the conduct it is associated with more of. Limit: correlational, three-level meta-analysis; the direction of causation cannot be fixed from these data alone. The file is `study-visser-2022-school-meta-analysis`.

**Gershoff, Sattler and Holden (2019)**, *Journal of Applied Developmental Psychology* 63:1-8, used propensity matching on more than 800 young adults from the nineteen states where school corporal punishment was legal at the time. Those who had received it in school had lower high-school grade point averages, more depressive symptoms, and greater intention to spank their own children. Sixteen percent of respondents reported school corporal punishment. Limit: retrospective self-report with matching, not randomization. The file is `study-gershoff-sattler-holden-2019`.

**Font and Cage (2018)**, *Child Abuse and Neglect* 75:29-40, followed 658 children for three years. Only physical abuse predicted declines in cognitive performance, but every form of physical punishment, including mild corporal punishment, predicted declines in school engagement. The fact file's instruction is to claim disengagement from this study and not test scores. The file is `study-font-cage-2018`.

**Elgar and others (2018)**, *BMJ Open* 8(9), an ecological study of eighty-eight countries and 403,604 adolescents, found that countries with full bans on corporal punishment had lower rates of frequent physical fighting, and countries with school-only bans had less fighting among girls. Limit: ecological; the authors say causality is unclear. It is cited here for one purpose only: it is the best available evidence that bans are not followed by more violence. The file is `study-elgar-2018-bans-and-fighting`.

### 4.3 What the evidence does and does not establish

Taken together, the studies establish three things to the standard of a reasonable belief. The practice has no demonstrated benefit on any measured outcome, including the immediate one. It is consistently associated with more of the behavior it is meant to reduce. And the association holds in the school setting specifically, not only in homes.

They do not establish, and this brief does not claim, that a single paddling causes a particular child's later aggression, or that the effect sizes are large. An r of .27 is a moderate association. The argument does not need more. The privilege asks whether the actor reasonably believes the force is necessary, and a method with no measured benefit cannot be necessary when another method with measured benefit is at hand.

## 5. The equally effective means

Comment d asks whether "a less severe method appears likely to be equally effective." Three bodies of evidence answer it.

### 5.1 Controlled studies of classroom management

**Korpershoek and others (2016)**, *Review of Educational Research* 86(3), a meta-analysis of fifty-four controlled studies, found that classroom management programs improved students' academic, behavioral and social-emotional outcomes with an average effect of g = 0.22, with programs focused on social-emotional development contributing most. The Incredible Years Teacher Classroom Management program has randomized-trial support with small average effects (Reinke and others 2018, d about .13). Limit: effects are modest and depend on implementation. The file is `alt-classroom-management-korpershoek-2016`.

**Bradshaw, Waasdorp and Leaf (2012)**, *Pediatrics*, and **Bradshaw, Mitchell and Leaf (2010)**, *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions*: in a five-year randomized trial across thirty-seven Maryland elementary schools, School-Wide Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports reduced suspensions and office referrals, and children in PBIS schools were thirty-three percent less likely to receive an office discipline referral. Limit: effects depend on fidelity, most trial evidence is elementary, and PBIS is a framework, not a package; adopting the name without the tiers, data and coaching does not reproduce the results. The file is `alt-pbis-bradshaw-rcts`.

**Kellam and others (2008)**, *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*: children randomized to classrooms using the Good Behavior Game in first and second grade had, by ages nineteen to twenty-one, lower rates of drug and alcohol disorders, regular smoking and antisocial personality disorder, with the largest effects among the boys who were most aggressive in first grade. The game is free. Limit: a replication with less teacher coaching showed smaller effects. The file is `alt-good-behavior-game-kellam`.

The contrast with section 4 is the whole argument. The milder methods have randomized trials showing reductions in the behavior at issue. Corporal punishment has no trial, no measured benefit, and a consistent association with more of that behavior.

### 5.2 The boards that manage without it

As of October 29, 2026, this project's district records for the seventeen states whose law permits corporal punishment list 1,190 school boards with a written policy prohibiting it, against 1,475 whose policy permits it and 200 that permit it only with a parent's written consent. In Kentucky and North Carolina every district has prohibited it by policy although the statute still permits it. Nationally, 19,851 students received corporal punishment in 2023-24 in 832 districts, down from 69,492 in 2017-18. The files are `districts-prohibiting-boards-17-states-2026-10`, `law-states-permitting-count` and `crdc-national-total-2023-24-computed`.

The relevance is evidentiary. Comment d asks whether a milder method "appears likely" to be equally effective. The experience of more than eleven hundred boards in the same states, under the same statutes, with the same students, is direct evidence that schools can be run without it, and no study has found that any of them became more disorderly for the choice.

### 5.3 The professional consensus

The 2023 American Academy of Pediatrics policy statement recommends that corporal punishment in all school settings be abolished in all states by law and replaced by alternative forms of behavior management (`org-aap-2023-school-statement`). The National Association of School Psychologists "strongly opposes the use of corporal punishment in schools" (`org-nasp-position`). In November 2016 seventy-nine organizations, including the American Psychological Association, the American Federation of Teachers, the National Education Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the NAACP and the National PTA, signed an open letter organized by the National Women's Law Center calling for an end to the practice (`org-nwlc-open-letter-2016`). On March 24, 2023 the United States Secretary of Education wrote to every governor and chief state school officer asking states to end it (`law-cardona-letter-2023`).

A reasonable belief is measured against what the relevant professional community believes. Here the relevant community, pediatricians, school psychologists, and the two teachers' unions, is on record that the practice is not necessary and should stop.

## 6. The comparative decisions

Two apex courts have read a reasonableness standard against this kind of evidence. Neither is binding on any American court. Both show that the exercise is a judicial one and how it comes out.

### 6.1 Canada: Canadian Foundation for Children, Youth and the Law v. Canada, 2004 SCC 4

Section 43 of the Criminal Code of Canada excuses a schoolteacher or parent who uses force "by way of correction" if the force "does not exceed what is reasonable under the circumstances." The Supreme Court of Canada upheld the section against a Charter challenge, but only after giving "reasonable under the circumstances" a content drawn from the expert evidence. At paragraph 37 the majority recorded the experts' agreement that corporal punishment of children under two, of teenagers, with objects, or to the head is harmful and "will not be reasonable." At paragraph 38:

> Contemporary social consensus is that, while teachers may sometimes use corrective force to remove children from classrooms or secure compliance with instructions, the use of corporal punishment by teachers is not acceptable. ... Substantial societal consensus, supported by expert evidence and Canada's treaty obligations, indicates that corporal punishment by teachers is unreasonable.

And at paragraph 40: "Teachers may reasonably apply force to remove a child from a classroom or secure compliance with instructions, but not merely as corporal punishment."

The court did not strike the statute. It read the word "reasonable" in light of the evidence and concluded that, for teachers, corporal punishment can never satisfy it. That is the move this brief asks an American court to make with § 147(2) and the state statutes, which use the same word. The file is `law-canada-2004-scc-4-teachers`.

### 6.2 South Africa: Freedom of Religion South Africa v Minister of Justice, [2019] ZACC 34

The Constitutional Court of South Africa, unanimously, declared the common-law defence of reasonable and moderate parental chastisement unconstitutional. The case concerned parents, not schools, and the drafter should say so. Its reasoning, though, is a model of how the necessity inquiry proceeds once a court takes it up. At paragraph 39 the Chief Justice asked "why it is necessary to resort to chastisement in the first place. Is it not the actual or potential pain or hurt that flows from it that is believed to be more likely to have a greater effect than any other reasonably available method of discipline? Otherwise, why resort to it?" At paragraph 64 he noted "the paucity of clear or satisfactory empirical evidence that supports chastisement as a beneficial means of instilling discipline," and he was candid that some of the harm research fails to separate moderate from abusive force. At paragraph 67: force "cannot in circumstances where there is an effective non-violent option available be said to be consonant with the best interests of a child." At paragraph 70:

> the paucity of proof that the chastisement is beneficial and the availability of less restrictive means to instil discipline militate against the reasonableness and justification of the limitation.

The two prongs of that sentence, no proof of benefit and an available milder means, are comment d. The file is `law-south-africa-2019-zacc-34`.

## 7. The American Law Institute's Restatement of Children and the Law

The ALI's *Restatement of the Law, Children and the Law* addresses the school privilege directly. Section 8.10 of Tentative Draft No. 2 (2019), "Use of Force to Control and Punish," reads:

> In a criminal proceeding or a civil suit alleging an intentional tort, the use of reasonable force by a teacher or other school authority with responsibility for the care and education of students is privileged if the school authority reasonably believes that the force is necessary to maintain order and safety in the school.
>
> The use of force to punish through the infliction of pain is not necessary to maintain order or safety, and therefore corporal punishment is prohibited in public schools unless expressly authorized by statute, and any such authorization is to be narrowly construed.

Comment c states the reasoning in the Restatement's own voice:

> Where a broad and growing legal, medical, and social scientific consensus categorically rejects the propriety and efficacy of corporal punishment, corporal punishment cannot be said to be "necessary." Thus, application of the "reasonable belief in necessity" requirement precludes application of the common-law privilege to corporal punishment.

Comment a adds that where corporal punishment is still authorized by statute, "the reasonableness contemplated in those statutes is increasingly out of step with trends in the law," and that even there "the reasonableness standard increasingly restrains the scope of that privilege."

Status. The ALI membership approved the full Restatement on May 17, 2024. The Institute's press release of that date says that "until the official text is published, the drafts approved by the membership are the official position of ALI, and may be cited as such." The published volumes are sold, not posted, and this project has not read the printed § 8.10; the wording above is the 2019 draft as the ALI posted it, and every citation should say so until someone checks the printed section. The file is `law-ali-restatement-children-8-10`.

The weight of the Restatement is persuasive, not binding, but it is the successor to the Restatement (Second) on which *Ingraham* relied, written by the same institution, and it reaches the conclusion this brief argues for by the same route: a reasonable belief in necessity cannot survive the evidence.

## 8. Application

### 8.1 The elements and the evidence

The privilege requires that the actor reasonably believe the force necessary. Separate the two words.

*Belief.* The teacher who paddles believes it will help. That belief is sincere and the argument does not question it.

*Reasonable.* A belief is reasonable when a careful person in the actor's position, knowing what that person knew or should have known, could hold it. The question is therefore what the actor knew or should have known. In 1977 a teacher could reasonably believe that paddling worked, because nobody had measured it. In 2026 the measurement has been done, seventy-five times for physical punishment in general and twenty-one times for school paddling in particular, and the answer each time is no benefit. A belief that contradicts every measurement is not a reasonable belief once the measurements are known to the believer.

*Necessary.* Comment d gives necessity its comparative content: force is not necessary where a milder method appears likely to be equally effective. The milder methods have randomized trials; the practice has none. More than eleven hundred boards in the same states use the milder methods alone. On this record no fact-finder could find that the harsher method was necessary in the comparative sense the Restatement requires.

### 8.2 What notice does to the element

The reasonableness of a belief depends on knowledge, and knowledge can be supplied. Three documents put a board on notice.

The 2016 open letter from seventy-nine organizations told every board that the medical, psychological and educator professions had concluded the practice should end. The Secretary of Education's letter of March 24, 2023 told every governor and chief state school officer the same, with the federal data attached. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2023 statement told every pediatrician, and through them every parent who asks, that the practice should be abolished in all schools.

A memorandum delivered to a particular board, such as the two-page memo that accompanies this brief, does something the general letters do not: it fixes the date on which this board, with these members, had the evidence in hand. From that date forward, a decision to keep the policy is a decision made with knowledge that the practice has no measured benefit and that milder methods work. The teacher who paddles under that policy acts on a belief the board has been told is unfounded. Whether that is pleaded as negligence in the board's policy-making, as the loss of the "reasonable belief" element of the individual privilege, or as the loss of a statutory immunity conditioned on reasonableness, the notice is the fact that changes the analysis.

The drafter should be precise about what notice does not do. It does not make a routine paddling malicious, and it does not, by itself, meet the shocks-the-conscience standard in the seven circuits. It goes to reasonableness and necessity, which are the state-law elements and the "need presented" element of the federal proportionality test.

### 8.3 Anticipated responses

*The legislature has authorized it, so it is reasonable per se.* No statute in the table says that. Each conditions its protection on reasonableness, necessity, good faith, or compliance with a policy that itself says reasonable. The Canadian court faced a statute that expressly excused corrective force and still read "reasonable" against the evidence. The ALI draft says the statutory authorization is "to be narrowly construed."

*The research is correlational.* It is, and the brief says so at every point. The standard, though, is reasonable belief, not proof of causation. A reasonable person does not need to know that paddling causes aggression to know that there is no evidence it reduces aggression, and the latter is what necessity turns on.

*Parents consent.* In Missouri, Louisiana and Florida they must, in writing. Consent bears on the parent's claim and on the parent's own privilege. It does not supply the teacher's reasonable belief in necessity, which is an element of the teacher's privilege, and the school's authority under compulsory education laws is independent of parental delegation, as the ALI comment a explains.

*Juries, not judges, decide reasonableness.* Usually. But where the undisputed evidence shows no benefit and an available milder means, the question of whether any reasonable fact-finder could find necessity is a question of law, and that is the question the comparative courts answered.

### 8.4 Assembling the record

The argument is only as strong as the record that carries it, and most of that record can be built before any case exists.

1. **The board's own words.** Obtain the district's current policy and the student handbook through a public records request and quote the sentence that conditions the practice: "reasonable," "only after other methods have failed," "reason to believe it will be helpful," or whatever the board adopted. The standard the board wrote is the standard it will be held to, and in Alabama and Arkansas it is the standard the immunity statute incorporates.

2. **The notice.** Deliver the memo that accompanies this brief to the board at a public meeting, with the studies attached, and keep the minutes. The date fixes the moment from which the board's belief is measured against the evidence. The 2016 open letter, the Secretary's 2023 letter and the Academy's 2023 statement show that the general notice was given years earlier.

3. **The district's own discipline data.** Office referrals, suspensions and corporal punishment counts by school and by year, from the district's records and from its Civil Rights Data Collection filings. If the district has schools that use corporal punishment and schools that do not, or years before and after a change, the comparison is the equally-effective-means evidence in the district's own numbers. No district has yet published this comparison; the first one to do so would produce the first United States before-and-after study (`evidence-no-post-ban-outcome-study`).

4. **The prohibiting boards in the same state.** This project's district file for the state, with each board's policy sentence and source URL. A list of the boards in the same athletic conference, the same county and the same size band that run without the practice is the most concrete form the comparative evidence can take.

5. **Expert declarations.** The authors of the meta-analyses in section 4 and the trials in section 5 can say in a declaration what their papers say in print, and can say in the same declaration what the papers do not show. A declaration that concedes the limits is worth more than one that overstates them, because the standard is reasonable belief and the question is what a reasonable actor would make of the evidence as it is.

6. **The insurer's answer.** A written question to the district's liability carrier, asked at a public meeting, whether corporal punishment is a rated exposure and whether the policy's intentional-acts exclusion reaches it. The answer, whichever way it goes, is part of what a reasonable board knows.

## 9. The venues

### 9.1 Where the argument is live

**State tort actions in permitting states.** This is the natural forum. The claim is battery; the defense is the privilege; the reply is that the privilege's reasonable-belief-in-necessity element is not met on this evidence. The immunity statutes in section 2.3 are pleaded by the defense and answered by their own words. Mississippi and Texas, whose statutes build necessity into the text, are the strongest; Georgia, with "good faith and not excessive or unduly severe," is next.

**Substantive due process in the seven circuits.** The "need presented" element of the proportionality test is where the evidence enters. The argument will not by itself make a routine paddling shock the conscience, and the drafter should not pretend it will; it narrows the range of force a court can call proportionate.

**The Fifth Circuit's en banc invitation.** Two judges of that court have urged reconsideration of *Fee*. The state-law argument is the way to test *Fee*'s premise that state remedies are adequate. A Texas, Louisiana or Mississippi trial court that applies § 9.62 or § 37-11-57 honestly, and finds the privilege unavailable on this evidence, proves the premise true; a court that refuses to apply its own statute's words proves it false.

**State constitutions.** Pressly Pratt, *Hitting Back*, 76 Fla. L. Rev. 789 (2024), argues from Florida's uniform-education clause (article IX, section 1) and privacy clause (article I, section 23). A state constitutional education clause is a plausible home for the argument that a practice with no educational benefit cannot be part of the system the clause guarantees. Pratt's piece is a student note and should be cited as such.

**Boards and insurers.** The memo accompanying this brief is addressed to a board. Where a district's insurer asks whether corporal punishment is a rated exposure, this brief is the answer to why it should be.

### 9.2 Where it is not

The argument does nothing under the Eighth Amendment, which *Ingraham* closed. It does not supply a federal claim in the Fifth Circuit under current law. It does not reach criminal prosecutions directly, because the criminal justification statutes track the same reasonable-belief standard and a jury instruction on necessity is a matter for the trial court; it can inform a charging decision. It does not apply to force used to restrain a dangerous student or to remove a child from a room: every authority in this brief, including the ALI draft and the Canadian court, preserves that force, and the model policy preserves it too.

## 10. The disability overlay

Five states prohibit corporal punishment of students with disabilities while still permitting it for other students: Louisiana (2017), Tennessee (2018, with a parental-permission exception), Arkansas (2019, four categories), Mississippi (2019) and Oklahoma (2025). Mississippi and Arkansas withdraw immunity for violations. The file is `law-disability-exemptions-by-state`.

The overlay matters to the necessity argument in two ways. First, these legislatures have already decided that for one class of students the practice is never necessary, which concedes the premise that necessity is a question the law can answer categorically. Second, in the states that still permit it for students with disabilities, the Restatement's own factor, "the physical and mental condition of the child," and the federal data showing that students with disabilities receive corporal punishment out of proportion to their numbers, make the necessity showing hardest for the defense where a disability is present. A paddling that a federal evaluation would have prevented, had the Tennessee checklist been followed, is a paddling that was not necessary on the statute's own terms. Section 504 and the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act supply parallel claims that this brief does not develop.

## 11. Conclusion

The common law never privileged force that was unnecessary. It privileged force that a reasonable teacher believed necessary, and it measured necessity by asking whether a milder method would likely have worked as well. For most of the privilege's history that question had no empirical answer. It has one now. The practice has no measured benefit; the milder methods do; more than eleven hundred boards use them alone. No United States court has been asked the question in these terms. This brief is the request that one be asked.
