# Memorandum: is corporal punishment necessary?

**To:** The Board of Education, [DISTRICT NAME] School District, [STATE]
**From:** EarthPilot Safe Schools Project
**Date:** [DATE]
**Re:** The board's own standard for corporal punishment, and what the evidence says about it

*This memo is a model for discussion. It is not legal advice and it is not a filing. Every figure in it is kept in a sourced fact file at earthpilot.org/kids/resources/. Bracketed items are filled in for each state and district.*

## The board's own standard

[STATE] law permits corporal punishment only when it is [STATUTE WORDS] ([STATUTE CITATION]). The district's own policy says: "[POLICY WORDS]." Behind both sits the rule the Supreme Court described in nineteen seventy-seven: a teacher may use the force a teacher "reasonably believes to be necessary" for the child's control, training or education, judged by all the circumstances, including "the availability of less severe but equally effective means of discipline."

## What each word asks

*Reasonable* asks whether a careful person, knowing what is now known, would choose this method. *Necessary* asks whether it does something no milder method would do. *Not excessive* asks whether it is the least force that gets the result. Where the policy says *only after other methods have failed* and *reason to believe it will be helpful*, it asks the board to compare methods and to expect a result. These are questions about results, and results can be measured.

## What the research answers

- **Does it make children behave?** A review of seventy-five studies covering one hundred sixty thousand children (Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor, two thousand sixteen) found physical punishment linked to thirteen of the seventeen outcomes measured, every one for the worse and none for the better. Immediate obedience, the one thing it is supposed to produce, was not significantly improved.
- **In schools?** A review of twenty-one studies of school corporal punishment covering sixty-seven thousand children (Visser, van der Put and Assink, two thousand twenty-two) found more aggression and disruption, more anxiety and depression, and lower school performance among the students who received it.
- **Later on?** A matched comparison of more than eight hundred young adults from the states where it was legal (Gershoff, Sattler and Holden, two thousand nineteen) found that those paddled in school had lower high school grades and more symptoms of depression.
- **Does it keep children engaged?** A three-year study of six hundred fifty-eight children (Font and Cage, two thousand eighteen) found that every form of physical punishment, including mild, predicted a drop in school engagement.
- **Do milder methods work?** A review of fifty-four controlled studies of classroom management programs (Korpershoek and others, two thousand sixteen) found that they improved behavior, learning and social skills. A five-year randomized trial in thirty-seven schools (Bradshaw and others, two thousand twelve) found one third fewer office discipline referrals under schoolwide positive behavior supports.
- **Does stopping make things worse?** No study has found that ending corporal punishment increases misbehavior, and no study has found that using it improves behavior (literature reviewed through September two thousand twenty-six).

Most of these studies show association, not cause, and this memo does not claim more. The board's standard does not require more. It requires a reasonable belief that the method is necessary, and a method with no measured benefit is not necessary when methods with measured benefit are at hand.

## Boards that manage without it

More than eleven hundred school boards in the seventeen states whose law still permits corporal punishment have a written policy prohibiting it. ## What the law's own authors say

The American Law Institute, whose Restatement of Torts supplied the standard the Supreme Court quoted in nineteen seventy-seven, approved a new Restatement of Children and the Law in May two thousand twenty-four. Its section on school discipline says: "The use of force to punish through the infliction of pain is not necessary to maintain order or safety." The American Academy of Pediatrics asked every state to end the practice in two thousand twenty-three. The United States Secretary of Education wrote to every governor asking the same in March of that year. Seventy-nine national organizations, including both teachers' unions, the NAACP and the National PTA, signed an open letter to the same effect in two thousand sixteen.

## What this means for the board

The board's policy and the state's statute protect only force that is reasonable and necessary. Before this memo, the board could say it did not know what the research found. After it, a decision to keep the policy is a decision to keep a harsher method where a milder one is known to work as well. That is the one thing the standard does not allow. It matters to whoever insures the district as well: an immunity conditioned on reasonableness is only as good as the reasonableness.

## The ask

Adopt the sentence that boards across [STATE] have already adopted: "Corporal punishment is prohibited in all schools and programs operated by the [DISTRICT NAME] School District." Keep every other tool the district uses. Nothing in that sentence limits the reasonable force a staff member needs to protect a student or others from harm, to take a weapon, or to keep order; the model policy that accompanies this memo says so in its next sentence.
